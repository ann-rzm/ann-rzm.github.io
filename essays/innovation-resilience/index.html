From Catch-Up Efficiency to Innovation Resilience

RESEARCH ABSTRACT

Overview

A macroeconomic account of the shift from catch-up efficiency toward innovation resilience under changing incentives, competition, risk, and strategic constraint.

—From Catch-Up Efficiency to Innovation Resilience

The Bottleneck in Raising Total Factor Productivity and the Failure of Incentives

The 2025 Nobel Prize in Economics once again placed theories of innovation-driven economic growth at the center of global attention. The three laureates—Joel Mokyr, Philippe Aghion, and Peter Howitt—provide classic analytical frameworks for understanding the sources of long-run economic growth, respectively emphasizing the institutional foundations of knowledge accumulation, the inverted-U relationship between competition and innovation, and the endogenous risks of “creative destruction.” As China begins the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan period (2026–2030), this body of theory offers a useful perspective for interpreting the economic transformation now under way in China.

At China’s current stage of economic development, the effectiveness of the traditional model of “muscle-driven growth”—expansion based on factor inputs—has weakened significantly. This means that the contribution of labor quantity to economic growth is gradually declining, while the scope for technological catch-up and resource reallocation is narrowing. Against this background, continually raising total factor productivity (TFP) and shifting the growth engine from quantitative expansion to qualitative improvement have become central to making China stronger as well as larger. Technological self-reliance has likewise shifted from an optional objective to a necessary precondition.

From the Fourteenth to the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan, the core framing of China’s economic strategy has evolved. Broadly, the emphasis has shifted from a development agenda toward the coordinated pursuit of security and development. A press conference following the Fourth Plenary Session of the 20th Central Committee stated that China should “fully leverage the institutional advantages of socialism with Chinese characteristics, the advantages of an ultra-large market, a complete industrial system, and abundant human resources, and turn these advantages into tangible results in high-quality development.” This shift marks China’s transition from a “catch-up” economy pursuing efficiency under established globalization rules toward a “builder” seeking both security and development in a world full of uncertainty. In substance, it incorporates risk premiums and security costs into the objective function of national development, constituting an active and forceful response to deglobalization and technological containment.

On the eve of the Fourth Plenary Session of the 20th Central Committee, a series of articles by the Central Financial and Economic Affairs Commission painted a picture of “certainty” and strengthened public confidence. Yet real challenges remain, including industrial divergence, “involutionary” competition, and dependence on others for key core technologies. At the micro level, firms and individuals commonly experience transformation as difficult. If the design is so certain and the objectives so clear, why does this much-anticipated transformation remain so challenging in practice?

The challenges described above are structural consequences of the shift from “efficiency first” to giving equal weight to security and development. In terms of growth drivers, the focus has moved from generalized innovation to “new quality productive forces” centered on frontier technologies. In domestic-demand strategy, the strong domestic market has been upgraded from a “strategic base” to a “strategic anchor” for responding to uncertainty. In industrial policy, the emphasis has moved from upgrading toward security-oriented consolidation, expansion, and stronger foundations and greater efficiency. In the relationship between government and markets, greater attention is being paid to combining an effective market with a proactive government in order to address “involutionary” competition. These changes mean that the core objectives and constraints of national development differ from those of the past. A strategy intended to improve long-term “dynamic resilience,” however, may also increase the short-term adjustment costs borne by micro-level actors and create new challenges for resource allocation.

At the micro level, the transformation is challenged primarily by insufficient incentives for firms to innovate. As traditional growth drivers weaken, improvements in total factor productivity (TFP) face bottlenecks; external technological restrictions also impede the route of raising TFP through imitation to some extent, making technological self-reliance and rapid iteration necessary strategic choices. Macro-level structural constraints are ultimately transmitted to firms and influence their innovation decisions. The Fifteenth Five-Year Plan policy framework is intended as a forward-looking, targeted response to these constraints, fundamentally seeking to correct micro-level incentives.

Micro-Level Incentives from a Macro Perspective

Macro Context: Three Constraints on Innovation-Driven Transformation

China’s current pursuit of innovation-driven transformation faces three fundamental macro-structural constraints, which form the background to problems at the micro level.

Constraint 1: Diminishing Globalization Dividends and Rising National Risk Premiums

In the past, Chinese firms largely grew in an environment where they pursued efficiency under established globalization rules. Today, major-power competition has become more complex, external uncertainty and instability have increased, and risks and challenges have multiplied. The Recommendations note that relations among major powers shape the evolution of the international situation, that changes in the international situation profoundly affect domestic development, and that China faces a period in which strategic opportunities coexist with risks and challenges and uncertain, difficult-to-predict factors are increasing.

Market analysis, however, suggests that “new growth drivers represented by the European Union and emerging economies in Africa and Latin America are gaining renewed momentum.” Year-on-year exports to economies participating in the Belt and Road Initiative grew by 17.2%, while exports to Africa and Latin America grew by 30.6% and 17.5%, respectively, indicating that China is further diversifying its partners. Against the backdrop of a U.S. economic slowdown and tariff barriers, trade with the United States continues to face pressure and has become a major drag. Efforts by the United States and other Western countries to regionalize or reshore supply chains, promote “friend-shoring,” and bring industries back home also expose China to additional risks and challenges. These developments have substantially increased the systemic risk facing firms’ innovation decisions, particularly external risk. Firms no longer operate in a stable free-trade environment; they must bear a higher “risk premium” for possibilities such as geopolitical conflict and supply-chain disruption.

Constraint 2: The “Ceiling Effect” in Technological Catch-Up by Latecomer Economies

China’s rapid growth in the past benefited substantially from the scope for technological catch-up and resource reallocation. As the country approaches the technological frontier, however, the space for imitation is narrowing, forcing firms to turn toward more uncertain independent exploration. This suggests that there remains room to improve basic research that reveals scientific principles. Without a strong foundation in basic research, original technological breakthroughs will be constrained. Firms undertaking exploratory innovation consequently face greater uncertainty, which may reduce their probability of success to some extent.

Constraint 3: Internalizing the Social Costs of “Creative Destruction”

“Creative destruction,” as proposed by Joseph Alois Schumpeter, is an important driver of economic growth. Yet rapid transformation in an ultra-large economy entails significant adjustment costs. Transformation therefore involves not only technological upgrading but also the mitigation and resolution of social costs, including those related to employment and regional development. Industrial transformation and upgrading do not proceed uniformly, so aggregate data and the experiences of some micro-level actors may diverge. Data indicate that wage growth in AI-related and other industries has reached twice the average rate in other sectors, while real estate-related industries have weakened in their capacity to absorb labor and have adjusted wage levels. Structural transition in an ultra-large economy thus creates internal social shocks that are also part of systemic risk.

Analysis Through the Lens of Nobel Laureates’ Theories

Mokyr’s perspective (affecting the probability of success):

Mokyr emphasizes that sustained innovation depends on knowledge accumulation and inclusive institutions. In China’s current circumstances, there remains room to improve basic-research investment and institutional openness at the macro level, which to some extent constrains firms’ expected probability of success in exploratory innovation. When an economy’s education system, research-evaluation mechanisms, and intellectual-property protections have not yet fully created an environment that encourages exploration, its most exploratory-minded talent may be unable to devote itself fully to unknown fields. Over the long run, this may affect the depth and diversity of the economy’s knowledge base and, in turn, systematically influence the probability of original innovation succeeding.

Changes in the probability of success affect different types of firms to different degrees. For leading private technology firms exploring the “uncharted territory” of frontier technologies—such as AI-chip and core-algorithm companies—weak basic research directly presents a major obstacle to overcoming the ceiling effect. Original innovation from zero to one carries an extremely high risk of failure, which can suppress firms’ willingness to pursue it. For traditional-industry firms and many small and medium-sized enterprises that mainly apply, adapt, and improve existing technologies, the impact on success rates is more indirect and is reflected primarily in higher technology-import costs and slower iteration.

Aghion and Howitt’s perspective (affecting profit margins):

Their theory identifies an inverted-U relationship between competition and innovation. “Involutionary” competition in parts of China’s economy is a micro-level manifestation of an imbalance in this relationship. Excessive competition in areas such as new-energy vehicles and the platform economy squeezes firms’ profits, weakens their capacity for long-term R&D investment, and can trap them in a cycle of low-level innovation. Firms experience pressure on profit margins in different ways. For the many small and medium-sized private firms in highly competitive industries, price wars directly and visibly erode already limited resources for innovation. For some firms with market power or specific barriers to entry, constraints on profit margins may arise more from administrative price regulation or social responsibilities; their lack of innovation incentives may instead stem from insufficient competitive pressure. Against this background, policies to regulate market competition aim to protect firms’ capacity to invest in innovation and restore competition to a healthy range that encourages it.

Schumpeter’s perspective (risk factors):

The theory of creative destruction itself contains risk factors. Under particular conditions, the social costs of macro-level transformation and external market risks are amplified simultaneously. Externally, firms face pressure from supply-chain disruption; internally, they bear employment and regional-development pressures arising from transformation. Together, these pressures substantially raise structural risks for firms and suppress their willingness to invest over the long term. Building a social safety net and diversifying markets are therefore not merely social-policy or foreign-trade measures. By managing the internal and external shocks of creative destruction, they can reduce systemic risk and repair innovation incentives.

Chapter Summary

Taken together, the challenges facing China’s innovation-driven transformation arise fundamentally from the systemic effects of macro-structural constraints on firms’ micro-level innovation incentives. The three macro-level constraints affect key variables in firms’ decisions, lowering expected returns from innovation and raising expected risks. As a result, the strategic orientation toward innovation-driven growth coexists with insufficient innovative vitality at the micro level, forming a core challenge in the current transformation.

III. Policy Interpretation: Macro-Level Solutions to Micro-Level Incentive Failures

The release of the Recommendations for the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan marks an important strategic adjustment in China’s economic policy, intended to improve the innovation environment for micro-level actors through macro-level measures. In fact, during the Fourteenth Five-Year Plan period, China’s economic leadership had already anticipated the need to create new development space based on an in-depth assessment of global trends. It explicitly called for “forming a strong domestic market and accelerating the establishment of a new development pattern in which domestic circulation is the mainstay and domestic and international circulations reinforce one another.” The Fifteenth Five-Year Plan period deepens and implements this strategy. The Fourteenth Five-Year Plan drew the blueprint and began opening up new space for domestic circulation; the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan provides an implementation plan for consolidating and expanding that space amid greater external pressure and a more urgent need for transformation.

Reducing Innovation Costs and Expanding the Market Base

To reduce innovation costs, the “energy and foundational-technology strategy” addresses the core cost structure of the future digital economy and goes beyond the traditional goal of ensuring energy security. Relevant strategic analysis argues that “electricity is important infrastructure for developing new quality productive forces,” while energy exports are a key means by which China participates deeply in global cooperation. In the age of artificial intelligence, computing power has become a core productive force, and electricity is the underlying cost of computing. Access to affordable, stable, and green electricity therefore means securing an important competitive advantage in the future digital economy. China’s strategic orientation is to provide an internationally competitive energy-cost base for AI and digital-industry development at home, while strengthening its influence over costs in the global digital economy through exports of new-energy technologies, power equipment, and potentially future energy sources such as nuclear fusion. This is a forward-looking strategy for positioning China in global industrial chains and directly affects firms’ innovation costs.

To expand market scale, the position of “building a strong domestic market” has been upgraded from a “strategic base” in the Fourteenth Five-Year Plan to a “strategic anchor” in the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan. This wording change is significant: it marks the conversion of the “ultra-large market” from an economic advantage into a strategic asset and emphasizes a growth model relying more on domestic demand, consumption, and endogenous momentum. The economic logic is that a unified and enormous domestic market can generate five effects: economies of scale, economies of scope, innovation and creation, spatial agglomeration, and development spillovers. Economies of scale and innovation and creation are especially important for micro-level innovation incentives. The former means that substantial domestic demand can spread R&D and investment costs over greater output, directly affecting firms’ innovation costs. The latter means that a broad market provides rich settings for commercializing and iteratively improving innovations; by expanding opportunities for experimentation and application, it raises the expected probability that exploratory innovation will succeed.

A unified national market also serves as a buffer against risk. One analysis argues that “the stronger China’s domestic demand, the better positioned it is to respond to external shocks such as tariffs.” Building a unified market and expanding domestic demand are therefore crucial supports for strengthening China’s bargaining power and risk-bearing capacity as the international trade environment changes. When external markets contract because of trade barriers, domestic demand can absorb part of the available capacity and significantly mitigate the impact of external shocks, while creating more favorable conditions for China in international competition. This fundamentally expands the market-scale parameter and provides a more predictable and stable basis for returns on innovation.

Increasing the Probability of Innovation Success and Protecting Innovation Returns

“Organized innovation” is a core measure for raising the probability of success, intended to use system-level capacity to compensate for current limitations in individual original-innovation capabilities. Some experience has already accumulated, including cases in which local industrial investment platforms facilitate the commercialization of research from the University of Science and Technology of China and productive services cover 320,000 factories. These practices illustrate two typical models: government acts as a platform builder connecting research with the market, while chain-leading firms use digital platforms to systematically integrate and improve the innovative capabilities of hundreds of thousands of small and medium-sized enterprises. This model echoes Mokyr’s idea of bringing craftsmen and scientists together, but operates at a new scale and level of organization. Platforms and networks distribute R&D risks and provide common-technology support, substantially increasing the expected probability of success for exploratory innovation—particularly among small and medium-sized enterprises—and strengthening their willingness to undertake highly uncertain innovation activities.

At the same time, policy explicitly calls for comprehensive governance of “involutionary” competition. This directly addresses the problem identified in Aghion’s theory: excessive competition weakens incentives to innovate. In practice, excessive competition in some fields has compressed corporate profits, reduced the capacity for long-term R&D investment, and created a vicious cycle of low-level innovation. Regulating market competition is intended to correct market failures and ensure that firms receive reasonable returns on their innovation investment. Research by Peking University’s National School of Development provides a policy reference: the key is to reduce expectations of implicit guarantees and strengthen market discipline. Empirical findings show that, once expectations of rigid repayment guarantees were broken, capital markets shifted from ownership-based preferences toward efficiency-based preferences. Among firms with similarly high efficiency, the investment rates of state-owned and non-state-owned firms no longer differed significantly in statistical terms. This suggests that correcting resource misallocation through financial reform can help curb excessive investment by inefficient firms—one source of “involution”—and preserve reasonable profit margins for efficient firms engaged in genuine innovation.

Only when firms are confident that their innovations can generate reasonable and sustained returns in the market will they have an incentive to redirect resources from price competition to R&D. This policy orientation seeks to maintain healthy profit margins and ensure the commercial sustainability of innovation, moving the economy away from the part of the inverted-U relationship in which competition suppresses innovation and toward the range in which it stimulates it.

Managing the Internal and External Shocks of Creative Destruction

To reduce external risk, “going global” and the Belt and Road Initiative have taken on new significance. In October 2025, the United States formally announced additional high tariffs on China. As traditional room for maneuver contracts amid the gradual adjustment of export and import figures affected by U.S. tariffs, market diversification has shifted from an optional measure to a necessary one. The strong 27.9% growth in China’s direct investment in Belt and Road partner countries from January through August 2025 indicates that preparations were already under way, proactively building a hedging mechanism against systemic risk.

China’s investment cooperation with Belt and Road partner countries, January–July 2025

A more notable change is structural: investment in information transmission, software, and information-technology services grew by 205.5%, indicating that the opening of “new tracks” is expanding application scenarios globally. “New space” is not merely geographic; it is also created by industries, and capital is actively flowing into fields representing new quality productive forces. The fact that non-state-controlled economic entities accounted for 54.3% of outbound investment provides evidence of, and a basis for validating, positive responses by micro-level actors.

MINISO’s successful entry into 112 countries and regions demonstrates the viability of an outward-expansion model combining China’s supply chain with a keen understanding of local markets. This opens alternative growth spaces for firms, directly reduces their exposure to external risks, and gives them room to adjust when a single market is hit, serving as an important buffer against external shocks.

To reduce internal risk, a sound social-security system serves a role beyond traditional welfare: it becomes a safety net that sustains economic stability during transformation. One important indicator is that the growth rate of inclusive loans to small and micro businesses exceeded 20%. This suggests that financial resources are being directed toward small and medium-sized business entities, which are highly dynamic but relatively vulnerable. A comprehensive system encompassing inclusive finance, social security, and related measures can buffer the social costs of creative destruction, including unemployment and income volatility, reduce systemic risk across the economy, and create a more stable environment for entrepreneurs’ long-term investment decisions.

Conclusion

At this new historical starting point, China’s path to innovation requires both the certainty provided by top-level design and the creativity generated by grassroots experimentation. When the institutional advantage of “concentrating resources to accomplish major tasks” interacts with the market vitality of “a thousand sails racing forward and a thousand trees competing in spring,” a sweeping picture of innovation begins to unfold. It is both a symphony of national strength and a concert of the efforts of countless individuals. If proactive planning and an effective market each play to their strengths, and the efficiency of catch-up and the resilience of exploration work in tandem, China’s innovation engine will gain tremendous momentum—not only winning the present but also helping define the future.

Finding an appropriate balance between the efficiency of “planned innovation” and the vitality of “exploration beyond the plan” is a question for China and for the world. It may provide a landmark answer for innovation economics and development theory, offering China’s response to the world. Innovation by a major country is an art of balance: it is both a great ship driven by the state, advancing through the “known world,” and a surging current driven by the market, opening up the “unknown.” The Fifteenth Five-Year Plan’s answer should allow the ship to travel steadily and the current to flow without interruption. China’s economic vessel will thus forge resilience that cannot easily be shaken amid the era’s stormy seas.

Research context
