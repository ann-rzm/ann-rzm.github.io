RESEARCH ABSTRACT

Overview

A policy study of how finance, knowledge flows, shared digital infrastructure, and data assets can strengthen industrial modernization and productive resilience.

I. Introduction

The Recommendations of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China on Formulating the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan for National Economic and Social Development, adopted at the Fourth Plenary Session of the 20th Central Committee (hereinafter, the “Recommendations”), place “building a modernized industrial system” and “Digital China” side by side as core strategic tasks. General Secretary Xi Jinping’s important discussions of new quality productive forces have provided direction for building a modernized industrial system. Recently, Vice Premier He Lifeng emphasized in his article “Developing New Quality Productive Forces in Light of Local Conditions” the need to “accelerate the formation of production relations better suited to new quality productive forces.” Requirements such as “deepening reform of the fiscal, tax, and financial systems” and “guiding more capital to precisely empower technological innovation and the real economy” are becoming key levers for realizing this ambitious vision.

China enjoys two inherent advantages: a “complete industrial system” and an “ultra-large market.” Yet the performance of the future industrial system will depend critically on the efficiency of knowledge flows and conversion within it. Against a backdrop of sustained increases in investment in scientific and technological innovation, one point of entry for solving the problem is to bridge the “last mile” in translating scientific and technological achievements into applications. Data connectivity and knowledge sharing between “chain-leading” firms and small and medium-sized supporting enterprises, as well as helping large numbers of traditional firms move beyond the cognitive barriers encountered during digital transformation and strengthening their willingness and confidence to transform, together constitute major practical challenges.

In pursuing the innovation-led development path set out in the Recommendations, the 2025 Nobel laureates in economics offer a theoretical framework for understanding the internal logic of knowledge-driven growth. The development model is gradually shifting away from selecting winners by favoring particular industries and toward building a digital ecosystem that efficiently produces, diffuses, and combines “useful knowledge.” When capital allocation becomes more oriented toward efficiency, society-wide innovative vitality can be fully released. At the same time, when the efficiency of knowledge allocation becomes a core factor in development, it can fundamentally improve the overall resilience and innovative capacity of the industrial system.

The driving force of modern economic growth lies in the deep integration of knowledge and disruptive innovation. Drawing on relevant theory and China’s circumstances, this article seeks to explore the internal relationships and mechanisms linking the optimization of financial-resource allocation, the facilitation of knowledge flows across industries, and improvements in the performance of the industrial system.

II. Three Areas for Improving the Flow and Integration of Industrial Knowledge

The Recommendations for the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan place “building a modernized industrial system” at the forefront. According to new growth theory, innovation outcomes depend on the creation and circulation of “useful knowledge.” The key to turning the momentum of macro-level strategy into actual development therefore lies in the effectiveness of knowledge flows at the micro level. In Mokyr’s theory, “useful knowledge” consists of two types: scientific principles that explain “why,” and practical instructions that explain “how.” The gap between these two types of knowledge currently creates bottlenecks in industrial performance in China. Promoting their deep integration and practical application involves three connected and mutually reinforcing stages.

A. From Principles to Technology

In General Secretary Xi Jinping’s important discussions of new quality productive forces, a substantial increase in total factor productivity (TFP) is a defining characteristic. Improving the effectiveness of translating scientific and technological achievements into applications is a key route to raising TFP.

A prominent obstacle in translating principles into technology is a “financing gap.” Under intense market competition, firms’ innovation decisions are strongly affected by investment payback periods. They urgently need practical “operating instructions” that can directly reduce production costs and improve efficiency. Because investment risks are high and returns take a long time to materialize, financing gaps often arise at both the beginning and the end of the translation process between university research and firms’ technological needs.

Resource-allocation inertia associated with ownership has, to some extent, affected the efficiency of financial-resource allocation—that is, where capital flows—and slowed the integration of knowledge. Research on the credit-bond market by Peking University’s National School of Development quantified the cost of “implicit guarantees”: when market discipline strengthened, the difference in bond-issuance spreads between high- and low-efficiency firms narrowed by more than 15 basis points. This means that a high-quality firm issuing RMB 1 billion in bonds could directly save more than RMB 1.5 million in annual financing costs. This directly reflects a market pricing “efficiency” rather than “background.” The investment rate also rose by 0.22 percentage points, confirming that lower financing costs can quickly translate into actual innovation investment.

Under expectations of “implicit guarantees,” the credit-bond market has tilted markedly toward less efficient entities. This may obstruct the flow of credit resources that could otherwise support R&D and commercialization toward more efficient firms. Highly efficient firms working on the front lines of commercialization face the practical challenges of difficult and expensive financing, owing to structural problems such as narrow financing channels and insufficient credit histories and collateral. This, in turn, implicitly reduces the expected economic returns from the entire process of knowledge integration.

As market defaults occur and discipline strengthens, capital is flowing back toward more efficient firms. This is reflected in a marked narrowing of issuance-spread differences between high- and low-efficiency firms. Among high-efficiency firms, the investment-rate gap between state-owned and private firms has largely disappeared. This further supports the importance of breaking implicit guarantees so that capital can be guided away from judging firms by identity and toward judging them by efficiency.

Although challenges remain, practical experience has produced ways to break through them. A common approach is to embed structured financial engineering deeply into knowledge production and integration, seeking to bridge the critical path from intangible “knowledge” to “assets” that can be priced and traded.

Case 1: University of Science and Technology of China—An “Option” for Moving from the Laboratory to the Production Line

The University of Science and Technology of China has explored an innovative model for commercializing scientific and technological achievements that combines the allocation of rights, transfer, and agreed returns. It is analogous to the university issuing a “call option” to a research team: the team obtains ownership of the achievement, while the university retains certain rights to future value appreciation. The design has several theoretical advantages. First, linking the university’s returns closely to the future market value of the achievement helps address the difficulty of early-stage valuation. Second, clearly defining the research team’s rights and responsibilities can incentivize and sustain the industrialization of research results. Third, clear intellectual-property ownership and return expectations create more favorable conditions for risk-capital investment. This model can thus be regarded as a meaningful institutional innovation for addressing financing at the “first mile” of commercialization.

Case 2: Black Lake Technologies—From “Craft Experience” to “Data Assets”

Black Lake Technologies’ industrial-internet platform connects the production processes of tens of thousands of factories and aggregates large volumes of data in real time, ranging from production workflows to equipment status. This “data-rich resource” creates new possibilities for financial innovation; continuous data flows themselves hold substantial potential as new risk-control tools. In theory, financial institutions could use these data to tailor financial products for small and medium-sized manufacturers on the platform, linking credit limits and interest rates to the value of firms’ data assets—for example, improvements in production efficiency and order stability. In practice, one important reason some industrial funds invested in Black Lake was their confidence in the future value of its “industrial digital ecosystem.” They anticipated that it could serve as an entry point for expanding into broader financial markets, including supply-chain finance and equipment-financing leases. In other words, financial services could potentially expand from an individual factory to an entire digital production network.

These cases suggest that the ability of the financial system to serve new quality productive forces is closely related to whether it can move beyond a “pawnshop” model that traditionally scrutinizes financial statements and relies on collateral toward a new “cognitive” model that seeks to understand knowledge flows, assess knowledge value, and explore ways to share knowledge-related risks. A potential direction suggested by these cases is to use financial-engineering principles to design sustainable business models for knowledge circulation, with the aim of easing deeper structural frictions.

B. Knowledge Sharing and Joint Capability Building

General Secretary Xi Jinping has emphasized that “the principal role of enterprises in scientific and technological innovation must be strengthened.” This concerns not only individual firms but also the innovation ecosystem of the entire industrial chain. At present, gaps in knowledge coordination remain between chain-leading firms and their supporting enterprises.

The core issue is that industrial-chain coordination requires the financial system to price and incentivize “knowledge sharing” effectively. In practice, core firms worry that sharing core technologies may weaken their competitive advantage. Supporting firms, meanwhile, are uncertain whether their knowledge contributions will be fairly rewarded. Together, these concerns slow the flow of knowledge along the industrial chain and make it difficult for the financing of any single firm to strengthen the competitiveness of the chain as a whole.

This suggests that, while ensuring static “supply-chain security,” financial services also face an inherent need to promote the dynamic expansion of “knowledge-chain coordination.” This aligns with the strategic direction of the Recommendations. To implement the directives to strengthen enterprises’ principal role in technological innovation and improve industrial- and supply-chain resilience, finance for innovation chains may be a promising approach. For example, financing costs for chain-leading firms could be linked closely to the overall innovation performance of the industrial chain, measured by indicators such as the number of collaborative R&D projects or jointly held patents. In theory, this could create positive incentives to open knowledge interfaces. Building on reliable collaboration data accumulated by platforms, more precise credit services for supporting enterprises could also be explored. Although large-scale application still depends on further improvements in data compliance, risk-control models, and related conditions, the goal is to allow intangible knowledge value to circulate within the chain and be converted into tangible financial support.

C. Lowering the Barriers and Costs of Digitalization

Traditional industries account for 80% of manufacturing value added and are central to building a modernized industrial system through intelligent and green transformation and upgrading. Yet when these industries integrate with digital technology, they may exhibit hesitation about the pathways and outcomes of transformation—in other words, a “cognitive gap.”

Under the current new circumstances of economic and social development, the main barriers to digital transformation for traditional firms are high upfront costs and long transformation cycles. Firms widely seek long-term capital that shares risks and allows them to share in the gains from development. Breaking away from standardized product offerings and innovatively providing long-term capital could strengthen firms’ resolve to undertake digital transformation and promote high-quality economic development by unleashing vitality at the micro level.

With clear policy guidance, financial institutions can more effectively empower core firms through innovative financial products and services while advancing their own development. To structurally reduce firms’ financial barriers and perceived risks during transformation, technology and finance could be combined to stimulate firms’ innovative vitality and support high-quality industrial development.

III. Deeply Integrating Financial Support for a Modernized Industrial System with a Digital Ecosystem

The Recommendations for the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan state that an organic unity between an effective market and a proactive government should be established. In using finance to empower an industrial knowledge ecosystem, a basic prerequisite is to clarify the respective boundaries of government and market functions. Each bottleneck in the flow of industrial knowledge is also a potential target for financial innovation.

A. Making Knowledge an Asset That Can Be Priced

Historical experience shows that the essence of the Industrial Revolution lay in a virtuous cycle of interaction between scientific principles and practical technologies. Today, university research and firms’ technological needs in China resemble two groups speaking different languages, lacking an efficient “translation language” for communication. Bridging the “last mile” in the commercialization of research requires public spaces where common industrial knowledge can be accumulated, integrated, and disseminated.

Vice Premier He Lifeng has emphasized developing new quality productive forces in light of local conditions and guiding more capital to precisely empower technological innovation. Gradually reducing preferential biases in the allocation of financial resources is therefore an intrinsic requirement for developing new quality productive forces and improving capital efficiency. An effectively functioning and neutral “knowledge commons” would be valuable because it is equally open to all firms. Financial support is indispensable to moving this concept into practice: support must correspond to the value of knowledge rather than being influenced by an entity’s background, so as to ensure fairness and effectiveness. The analytical question is how much attention is paid to the value and potential of “knowledge” itself.

Beijing’s experience provides an example. The Guidelines for the Development of Common Technology Platforms in Beijing define such platforms as “functional vehicles that provide common-technology R&D and application services in response to industrial technology needs, and that serve as key infrastructure for industrial technological innovation.” The guiding principles reflect the combination of an effective market and a proactive government. Market-oriented operating mechanisms such as “those who participate benefit” and “those who invest benefit” are highly consistent with the core principles of openness, sharing, and incentive compatibility. This provides a useful benchmark for financial institutions seeking to identify and support foundational knowledge infrastructure that generates positive externalities.

For financial institutions, common-technology platforms represent a potential investment area. Foundational knowledge, such as open-source algorithms and standard datasets, has public-good characteristics. Firms’ proprietary core technologies, by contrast, should be priced and allocated appropriately through market-based financial instruments. This can reduce the cost of accessing common knowledge and create fertile ground for differentiated innovation by firms.

According to Aghion’s theory of competition, firms carefully weigh private costs and benefits when deciding whether to share knowledge. In theory, incentive mechanisms need to go beyond one-off data transactions and build an ecosystem that includes long-term rights, such as revenue sharing and priority access to joint R&D. The aim is for contributors’ marginal returns to cover—and exceed—the marginal competitive risks arising from knowledge spillovers, allowing them to obtain tangible returns from the ecosystem’s long-term prosperity.

Privacy-computing technologies such as “data usable but not visible” are foundational to sharing. Technology alone, however, does not directly create economic value. Realizing that value depends on external mechanisms, leaving room for finance to play a role: clear definitions of data or intellectual-property rights, enforceable benefit-sharing rules, and trustworthy dispute-resolution mechanisms. Whether an ideal model can operate reliably depends on effective integration of computing technology and institutional design. Technology is the tool; institutions provide the setting in which the tool can generate economic value.

B. From “Supply-Chain Efficiency” to “Innovation-Chain Resilience”

As discussed above, overcoming coordination barriers between chain-leading firms and supporting enterprises creates an inherent need to upgrade traditional supply-chain finance. A possible future direction is an “innovation-chain” model that can precisely support collaborative innovation across the industrial chain. In theory, enforceable cooperation contracts and benefit-sharing mechanisms can turn a potential zero-sum game into a predictable positive-sum game. A central consideration is how to improve the innovative capacity of the industrial chain as a whole. To move from technological monopoly toward ecosystem leadership, chain-leading firms must benefit tangibly from sharing, thereby linking their own interests to the overall innovative capacity of the chain.

From the perspective of contract theory, small and medium-sized firms with limited bargaining experience seek to reduce transaction costs in collaborative innovation. Standardized tools can lower the barriers to cooperation and reduce these transaction costs, clearing obstacles to collaborative innovation and enabling it.

C. Reducing Transformation Uncertainty Through Demand Orientation

One element in strengthening traditional firms’ resolve to transform and upgrade is using financial instruments to ease their concerns about transformation costs and market prospects. One of finance’s core functions is to translate national strategic intent into expectations that are stable, predictable, and perceptible to the market. In promoting firms’ transformation and upgrading, this is reflected in two dimensions.

First, certainty can be shaped at the strategic level. Under the guidance of industrial authorities, technological standards and investment road maps can be established. Financial institutions can then design appropriate financing instruments, providing strong, explicit incentives for firms that follow the development path. In effect, this sends society a credible signal about future technological directions. Such a signal can adjust expectations about short-term market fluctuations and provide a more certain guide for long-term social-capital investment, thereby stabilizing overall investment sentiment.

Second, practical problems can be addressed at the implementation level by diversifying risk. Highly concentrated risk is a major obstacle to firm transformation. Non-systemic risks originally concentrated in a single firm can be finely packaged and redistributed to core industrial-chain firms and market capital better able to bear and price them, thereby improving the allocation and dispersion of risk.

Conclusion

At the beginning of the new journey under the Fifteenth Five-Year Plan, economic theories associated with Mokyr and Aghion, together with micro-level evidence from China’s credit-bond market, point toward a clear trend: continued deepening of financial reform, more efficient resource allocation, and construction of a digital ecosystem serving knowledge integration may be one way to strengthen the foundations of new quality productive forces while following the objective laws of economic development. It follows that continually improving the effective combination of financial resources and knowledge factors is important to building a resilient modernized industrial system in China.

Research context

This paper develops its central question through a defined mechanism, explicit methodological boundaries, and a concluding account of its public significance.
