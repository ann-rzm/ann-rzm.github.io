RESEARCH ABSTRACT

Overview

A research design tracing how township-enterprise wages could shift women’s household bargaining power, children’s education, and China’s long-run human-capital formation.

Research Roadmap

This paper traces a proposed causal sequence in reform-era rural China: township-enterprise employment monetized women’s labor; wages improved women’s fallback position within the household; stronger bargaining power changed the allocation of family resources; and repeated investments in children’s schooling contributed to a broader expansion of human capital. The argument begins at household scale and asks how millions of small decisions could accumulate into national transformation.

The project does not treat one family history as proof of a national effect. It uses that history to identify a mechanism and then specifies the archival, oral-history, and quantitative evidence required to test it. The analytical contribution is to connect feminist household economics with political economy accounts of township and village enterprises.

1. Introduction: Why One Paycheck Matters

Accounts of China’s early reform period often emphasize decentralization, foreign investment, entrepreneurship, and the rapid growth of township and village enterprises. These explanations identify major institutions, but they can overlook the household decisions through which industrial change became durable social capacity.

The central question is therefore not only how rural factories generated output. It is how paid work altered the position of women whose labor had previously been absorbed into agriculture and household production without an independent wage. A paycheck could change who controlled cash, whose preferences entered family decisions, and whether scarce resources were directed toward immediate consumption or children’s education.

2. Historical Setting and Institutional Choice

At the beginning of reform, China faced limited urban employment capacity, large rural labor surpluses, fiscal pressure on local governments, and institutional restrictions on migration. Township and village enterprises offered a politically manageable form of industrialization: they created employment near existing communities, generated local revenue, and operated within collective or ambiguously defined ownership structures.

Near-home employment was especially important for women. It reduced the reputational, caregiving, and mobility costs associated with migration while allowing factories to recruit labor for textiles, assembly, light manufacturing, and other forms of production. The same arrangement that constrained mobility could therefore create a first opening into paid work.

3. From Labor Monetization to Bargaining Power

Household bargaining models predict that preferences receive greater weight when a member has stronger outside options and control over resources. Women’s wages need not have been large to matter. Regular cash income could reduce complete dependence on a husband or senior family member and make a woman’s contribution visible in a form recognized by both the household and local institutions.

The mechanism should not be romanticized. Paid work could add factory labor without reducing domestic responsibilities; wages could still be appropriated by the household; and occupational segregation could trap women in low-paid jobs. The claim is conditional: where women retained meaningful control over earnings, their bargaining position was more likely to improve.

4. Education as Household Reallocation

The next step concerns spending. If women’s income increased their decision-making weight, educational expenditure may have risen through school fees, books, tutoring, nutrition, or the willingness to keep a child in school rather than withdraw that child for work. Education functions here as a household public good whose benefits arrive slowly and whose allocation reflects bargaining priorities.

This mechanism requires evidence that separates wage effects from simultaneous changes such as state school expansion, fertility decline, urbanization, and rising household income generally. The strongest design would examine whether local exposure to township-enterprise employment produced larger educational changes in households where women entered paid work and controlled a greater share of income.

5. Sources and Methods

The quantitative strategy would combine county-level measures of township-enterprise intensity with census, household, and education data. Staggered local expansion could support a difference-in-differences design, provided that pre-trends and policy timing are carefully tested. Measures should distinguish total industrial growth from female employment and should report regional heterogeneity rather than assuming a uniform national effect.

The qualitative strategy would use oral histories, township payroll records, local gazetteers, Women’s Federation materials, family artifacts, and school-payment records where available. Interviews should reconstruct who received the first wage, who controlled it, which purchases became newly possible, how disagreements were resolved, and whether educational decisions changed. Claims would be graded by corroboration rather than by narrative vividness alone.

6. From Households to Human Capital

The macroeconomic argument depends on aggregation. A small increase in schooling within one household is not a national transformation. But if a similar mechanism operated across many industrializing rural counties, repeated educational investments could enlarge the cohort capable of entering more skill-intensive manufacturing and later service-sector work.

Any estimate of that contribution must remain modest. The paper does not claim that women’s wages alone produced China’s human-capital growth. It asks whether household bargaining supplied one underexamined micro-foundation through which industrial employment, education, and later productivity reinforced one another.

7. Comparative and Political Limits

The mechanism combines potentially general relationships—income, bargaining, and investment—with institutions specific to reform-era China, including collective land, household registration, village governance, and local state incentives. Comparison with other Asian development paths can clarify which effects arose from wage employment itself and which depended on this institutional configuration.

The same model also generated costs: uneven regional opportunity, gendered occupational segmentation, a double burden of paid and unpaid labor, and dependence on local authorities. A credible account must treat these costs as part of the institution rather than as exceptions to an otherwise uncomplicated success story.

Conclusion

The Younger Sister’s Tuition reframes industrialization as a sequence of decisions inside households as well as factories. Its proposed contribution is a testable mechanism linking women’s paid work to bargaining power, educational allocation, and long-run capacity. The project’s standard of proof is deliberately demanding: personal memory identifies the question, but only corroborated historical and quantitative evidence can establish the scale of the answer.

Research context

This paper develops its central question through a defined mechanism, explicit methodological boundaries, and a concluding account of its public significance.
