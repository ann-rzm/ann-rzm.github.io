RESEARCH ABSTRACT

Overview

An early reading of Kant’s perpetual peace through reason, law, unsocial sociability, and the institutional conditions that might turn conflict toward a cosmopolitan order.

In the early twentieth century, the doctrines of the German philosopher Immanuel Kant had already begun to exert a profound influence on Chinese academia. In particular, the dissemination and reception of his philosophical thought became an academic mainstream that could not be ignored. Wang Guowei, an outstanding representative of Chinese academia, was a model deeply influenced by Kantian philosophy. Yet although a hundred years have passed, we should still maintain an attitude of humility: our comprehensive understanding of Kant’s philosophy remains insufficient and still requires deeper investigation and study.

Specifically, when interpreting Kant’s philosophical thought, we often face many difficulties and challenges. For example, how Kant constructed and expounded his idea of universal history and his theory of achieving perpetual peace are questions that urgently require deeper excavation. In Kant’s political philosophy, the essay Perpetual Peace, whose English title is literally “Toward Perpetual Peace,” elaborates in detail Kant’s famous theory of maintaining peace through international political means. This theory profoundly reflects the core idea of Kant’s political philosophy—namely, that the world may ultimately develop in the direction of peace—and is also embodied in another work, Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Purpose.

Regarding this work of great value, some may misunderstand its importance or even question the depth of its core idea, simplistically regarding it as a vision, a suggestion, or a reverie—merely a fleeting pleasant topic among good-hearted people. I firmly oppose this view. I believe this core idea has extremely far-reaching significance. Like other great original conceptions, this idea can also be confirmed through rigorous argumentation—it is deeply rooted in the nature of reason and is the actual manifestation of reason’s absolute demand. Therefore, it should be included among those natural purposes that may be suppressed but can never be eliminated.

1. Analysis of Misconceptions About Perpetual Peace

Kant believes that human beings possess both reason and sensibility and exist simultaneously in two interwoven worlds. Yet reason occupies the dominant position therein; its superiority lies in its ability to overcome the blindness of sensibility and thereby construct a state of peace based on rational order. Reason, as the power governing the will, is described in the Metaphysics of Morals as follows: “The function of the will is to choose actions that are practically necessary (i.e., good) solely in accordance with reason, disregarding personal inclinations... The command of reason is the objective principle that the will must follow.” Sensibility often stems from the selfish natural disposition of human beings and, when dominant, easily gives rise to conflict and strife. By contrast, reason enables people to follow universal principles and achieve harmony between the individual and society. Therefore, the transition from sensibility to reason marks humanity’s crossing from imperfection to perfection—a process that drives human society from war toward peace, until the vision of perpetual peace is attained.

Kant takes the profound perspective of rational purpose as his starting point and prudently examines the evolution of human history. He is committed to exploring and seeking the universal laws of human social development, just as Newton revealed the mysteries of the natural world. He advocates taking rational principles as the guide, transcending the contingencies of historical development, and pointing out a clear and firm direction for human society. Kant profoundly perceives that human beings possess the dual attributes of sociality and unsociality. Therefore, the starting point of Perpetual Peace lies in the baseness of human nature, not in its purity and goodness. As the essay states at the end: “Human nature is a crooked timber that cannot be straightened.” Kant profoundly recognizes that many complex psychological traits exist in human society; we are not wholly innocent and pure. Yet he firmly believes that, against this background, achieving perpetual peace remains both possible and necessary. We hold firm hope in this. It is precisely through non-social factors such as vanity, the desire for power, and greed that humanity took its first step from the savage state toward civilized society and continues to advance along the path of enlightenment.

“This way of thinking can, over time, transform the crude natural capacity for moral discrimination into definite practical principles, and thus finally transform the pathological forced agreement to form a society into a moral whole.”

That is to say, as animal beings, the selfish tendencies in human nature often impel humans to abuse their freedom against their own kind. Therefore, when human beings live together, as rational creatures, we desire to define the boundaries of each person’s freedom through law. In this way, humanity will gradually construct a universally valid system of will that safeguards everyone’s freedom, thereby forming a lawful civil society and realizing universal lawful governance.

“Because the armed forces contending against one another exhaust all the powers of the commonwealth; because the devastation caused by war, and especially the need to maintain constant military preparedness, obstructs the full development of humanity’s natural capacities in their progress; yet, on the other hand, the disasters arising therefrom also compel our species to discover a law of equilibrium for dealing with the rivalry among states arising from their freedom (and healthy in itself), and to compel them to adopt a united power to reinforce this law, thereby leading to a cosmopolitan condition that safeguards international public security.”

Kant stands on this position and conceives that humanity will in the future construct a league among nations, building a grand commonwealth of states. Kant firmly believes that although the philosophical idea of a millennium of blessed peace may still seem distant, it is by no means a castle in the air but possesses substantive possibility. He further proposes the idea of an ethical community, which differs from a juridical community: it emphasizes adherence to inner moral laws rather than externally enforced universal laws.

The striking image presented at the opening—the words “Perpetual Peace” inscribed on a signboard at a church cemetery—itself, like many metaphors, contains elusive and multiply interpretable possibilities. As for the objective image and intellectual content Kant provides here, the purpose is to confuse the reader. Only through deep interpretation of Kant’s writing style and analytical understanding can we grasp its inner meaning with relative accuracy.

First, this image inevitably recalls certain ancient religious interpretations of the meaning of life: life is like a lodging house, or, more pessimistically, like a madhouse. Here, people not only sabotage their own goals but also inflict various sufferings on others, even regarding the capacity and skill for such behavior as supreme glory. From this perspective of religious doom, as long as humanity survives, mutual harm and the pleasure taken therein will never end, and the evolution of the techniques and power of such harm will never cease. This seems to be the fate of the human species. Perhaps humanity is the only species that will become extinct through mutual slaughter. Given that human existence is accompanied by war, the understanding of perpetual peace can, to some extent, only be reduced to the extinction of the human species. This view profoundly reveals a pessimistic outlook on life—namely, the belief that in this troubled world, true peace and tranquility cannot be found.

The essence of this view is a profound introspection on human history and reality. Since the dawn of civilization, human society has suffered from war, conflict, pain, and disaster. These historical memories give rise to deep questioning: does a world capable of achieving perpetual peace truly exist? When reality fails to provide a clear answer, some turn to fantasy, imagining that perhaps only when the human species no longer exists can true peace be realized.

However, such pessimistic speculation cannot provide a solution to the problem; instead, it may intensify human despair. In fact, the realization of perpetual peace is not without trace; the key lies in how we explore and construct it. We should change our perspective on life, no longer regarding peace as an unreachable dream, but actively striving for and safeguarding it through positive action. In this process, we must recognize that perpetual peace is not merely a political idea or social architecture, but a lofty aspiration of the human spirit. Only when every person’s heart is filled with peace and love can this ideal truly be realized. Therefore, we should dedicate ourselves to elevating individual moral cultivation and creating a social atmosphere of harmonious coexistence, with a view to realizing perpetual peace in human society.

In sum, although the concept of perpetual peace may seem remote in reality, this should not become a reason for us to abandon its pursuit. On the contrary, we should confront the challenges of life squarely and actively seek solutions, so as to achieve harmony and tranquility in human society. Only thus can we find true peace and happiness in this troubled world.

The vision of peace can only be entrusted to the afterlife. However, as a metaphor, Kant’s use of it does not stop at this superficial level; he adopts an ironic approach: thereby contrasting the profound implication of realizing perpetual peace in this world—if human society cannot achieve true perpetual peace, then “entering eternal rest” becomes humanity’s ultimate destination. This statement profoundly reveals the inner connection between humanity’s pursuit of peace and its ultimate destination, emphasizing the extreme importance of peace for human survival and development. We must, with firm determination and unremitting effort, advance human society toward the goal of perpetual peace.

The second interpretation of this image is closer to the original meaning Kant assigned to it—namely, that after some supreme power establishes universal despotic rule, all disputes subside and all things return to silence. This state can also be called a kind of peace. However, under such “peace,” the people face a double choice: either submit to high pressure and survive in humiliation, or rise in resistance and suffer brutal suppression. In Kant’s writings, the image of the “graveyard” is often used to depict the condition in which human freedom is thoroughly deprived under despotic rule. As stated in the Collected Writings on the Critique of Historical Reason:

(Nature) does not act like that kind of despotism (in the graveyard of freedom), which weakens all powers, but rather they arise and are secured in the balance of the most vigorous competition.

Under the shadow of despotic rule, all vital existence is ruthlessly stripped away, and any form of competition, including the most intense war, is thoroughly eliminated. The peace brought by such rule is achieved at the price of the total loss of human freedom. In short, this is a false peace under tyranny, in which the people take self-preservation as the basic goal of life and relinquish all freedom in exchange for absolute power’s maintenance of stability and order.

In a despotic system, the state and the ruler become the sole decision-makers, and the people must unconditionally obey their will; individual autonomy and voice are greatly restricted. This mode of rule not only shackles the people’s thought and behavior but also deprives them of basic rights and freedoms. The people are forced to abandon personal beliefs, values, and pursuits, becoming merely a component of the state machine. However, the peace under such despotic rule is not true peace, because it is built on the deprivation of the people’s freedom and dignity—a grave trampling of the people’s rights and dignity. In such peace, the people cannot feel true happiness and satisfaction, only oppression and pain.

In other words, despotic rule deprives the people of freedom and dignity, restricts their thought and behavior, and brings a false peace. This peace is achieved at the price of the total loss of the people’s freedom and dignity; it is a false peace shrouded in tyranny. Kant resolutely opposes such despotic rule, defends the people’s freedom and dignity, and pursues true peace and stability.

This is a peaceful life seeking security in servitude. However, Kant holds a negative attitude toward this “peace.” He believes that although war is unjust, the injustice of war has not yet reached the point where people must unconditionally endure all injustice in order to prevent its occurrence.

The third concept of “peace” that Kant rejects is embodied in the preliminary conditions he sets forth: namely, the “peace” fabricated by political maneuver, which we may also call the “peace” of politicians—a peace achieved through the use of stratagems and tactics to produce the appearance of a certain balance of power. In Kant’s view, this peace is merely an expedient or strategic means; it does not possess a true and enduring nature. Kant believes that this peace is a temporary compromise adopted by politicians under specific circumstances to safeguard their own interests.

The essence of this peace is a balance of power, achieved through politicians’ careful calculations and strategies. In this process, politicians employ various means, including negotiation, bargaining, and even secret backroom deals, to achieve a balance of power among themselves. This peace is not based on genuine mutual understanding and respect, but is a superficial, formal balance that may be shaken at the next moment by some new stratagem or strategy.

Kant points out that this peace is fragile; it depends on politicians’ wisdom and judgment, not on genuine moral and ethical principles. Therefore, this peace may collapse because of a momentary mistake or wrong decision. Moreover, this peace often leads to abuse of power and corruption, because politicians may sacrifice the public interest to maintain this balance of power. Kant believes that this politician’s “peace” is not peace in the true sense. It merely indicates that war has not yet broken out; in reality, it is an interlude of war. The Hobbesian state of nature certainly includes the outbreak of war, yet even if war is temporarily avoided through political maneuver, one has not truly escaped this state of nature. Regarding Hobbes’s exposition of the state of nature in his work, its profound connotation far exceeds the literal depiction of actual war scenarios. What Hobbes calls the “state of nature” essentially reveals a deeper and more universally existing state of interpersonal relations. On the surface, this state may give the illusion of peace, yet beneath this peaceful appearance lurks a profound hostility among people. The existence of this hostility, like a potential crisis, may be triggered at any time, making true peace an unattainable vision. Therefore, as long as people remain in this state of nature, true peace will always be an unreachable shore.

Kant profoundly points out that peace is a clear and unequivocal condition—namely, “the termination of all hostile actions.” Therefore, the prefix “perpetual” is in fact redundant. In his view, any state based on expediency, transience, preparation for war, or as a product of equilibrium or even a balance of terror is not a manifestation of true peace.

In sum, the perpetual peace Kant speaks of is neither the tranquility of the otherworldly realm, nor the complete disappearance of competition and the total annihilation of human freedom and vitality; it is not built on human indolence, despair, or mere survival, nor does it depend on the subjective goodwill of individuals or states, still less can it be achieved by politicians’ stratagems. Although the above factors may to some extent contribute to the appearance of peace—and some have indeed become the primary means of achieving peace in real politics—they have no direct connection with the concept of peace Kant expounds.

2. Positive Theoretical Explanation of Perpetual Peace

Kant profoundly points out that the state of nature is a stage in which juridical relations are not fully secured. To eliminate the universal hostility existing in this state and achieve enduring peace, the only way is for all people to jointly enter the track of a lawful society. The comprehensive construction of a juridical condition is the cornerstone of stable peace.

Kant decomposes the state of perpetual peace into three dimensions—the state, the international, and the world—specifically manifested as:

1. Civil law (domestic law), aimed at regulating relations among people within a state;

2. International law, aimed at coordinating interactions and relations among states;

3. Cosmopolitan right, aimed at handling the interactive relations between individuals and states within the universal “state” of humanity.

Kant emphasizes that these three juridical relations must be established simultaneously, to ensure that if any one of them remains in the state of nature, war cannot be avoided. And the three definitive articles ensuring perpetual peace likewise correspond to the two themes of Kant’s political philosophy. Each will be discussed in depth below.

The first topic focuses on the lawful state, whose core idea is to construct a constitutional state—that is, in the exercise of political power, it must be strictly constrained and regulated by law, ensuring the legitimacy and validity of state power. Kant emphasizes that for all modern governments, regardless of their form of government, their architecture must strictly follow the principle of the rule of law and construct a constitutional state bound by law, so as to ensure the normativity of political operation. In the course of deeply examining the Metaphysics of Morals, its core thrust is to clearly define the scope covered by the rule of law and the domain beyond its boundaries, aiming to clearly explain the essential distinction between the two.

In current legal studies, especially research in legal philosophy, we observe that legal scholars—especially professors at major law schools—have conducted in-depth research on Kant’s political philosophy. They are committed to exploring Kant’s philosophy of law, which constitutes an important component of Kant’s political philosophy.

It is worth noting that the German philosopher Hegel, in his Philosophy of Right, systematically inherited and deepened Kant’s exposition of legal philosophy within political philosophy. This highlights the core position of the idea of the lawful state in Kant’s political philosophy. Kant’s philosophy of law emphasizes that a lawful state should take law as its cornerstone and achieve the goals of state governance and social management through legal means. This idea not only has important guiding significance for legal research but also provides valuable intellectual resources for building a socialist state under the rule of law.

In Kant’s political philosophy, another important link lies in his view that we can effectively address and resolve various problems in the political domain by deeply analyzing the trajectory of historical development and through prudent reflection on the philosophy of history. Given that history is continuously evolving and developing, we should think deeply from the evolution of history about which problems need to be continuously resolved in logic and thought. Through in-depth study of the historical process, we can discover that the various problems in history are not fixed and unchanging, but are gradually properly resolved in the course of history. This manifests both the developmental nature of history and the continuous progress of human society through the long river of history.

Kant, as an outstanding philosopher, produced profound works in multiple historical periods. In 1784 he wrote Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Purpose, exploring historical ideas from a global perspective; in 1786 he completed Conjectural Beginning of Human History, providing a unique perspective for understanding the development of human history; in 1795 he published Toward Perpetual Peace, offering profound insights for seeking peace and stability in human society; subsequently, in 1798, he completed The Conflict of the Faculties, further enriching his political philosophy system. These works all focus on the core theme of the philosophy of history, embodying Kant’s outstanding contribution in the field of political philosophy.

In the political domain, the second major problem facing humanity is undoubtedly war. Kant once profoundly pointed out that war, as such a major issue, requires us to deeply reflect on the course of historical evolution and to clarify the goal of historical development in order to seek a solution. The cruel reality of war makes humanity profoundly understand the value of reason and morality, and then, under the guidance of the rule of law, jointly construct and maintain a peaceful social order. We can draw on the power of the philosophy of history to pursue the lofty goal of realizing perpetual peace. Therefore, Kant not only has a far-reaching influence in the field of philosophy, but his thought is also devoted to comprehensively transforming the inner world of human beings and reshaping humanity’s most basic and essential cognition of the world. At the level of political philosophy, he is committed to solving the most intractable political problems of human society, striving to safeguard social justice through comprehensive law-based governance and to resolve war crises through the wisdom of history.

As stated above, Kant’s political philosophy encompasses two core aspects: the rule of law and history. From the perspective of the rule of law, Kant emphasizes that we need to construct a republican government to achieve justice and order in political life; from the perspective of history, Kant’s intellectual achievements, based on the vision of eliminating war and achieving perpetual peace, are closely connected with our real life and possess important guiding significance. Looking back at the twentieth century, the series of international organizations we created—such as the International Court of Justice—and those arbitration institutions transcending the national level, have their core ideas and frameworks deeply rooted in Kant’s philosophy. The establishment of these organizations not only reflects the common vision of the international community but also demonstrates humanity’s unremitting pursuit of peace, justice, and cooperation.

In sum, Kant, as the founding father of the spirit of the United Nations, has far-reaching practical significance for contemporary society through his political philosophy. In other words, Kant’s political philosophy can be highly summarized by two concepts that still widely exist in reality today: republican government and international organization.

In the famous essay Idea for a Universal History with a Cosmopolitan Purpose, the view on achieving perpetual peace is explored in depth. The essay proposes nine propositions, the core content of which aligns with the series of views I have just expounded, pointing out the direction for us to pursue world peace and stability.

First, Kant once stated that nature endows every species with specific capacities, and the meaning of its existence lies in continuously developing and perfecting these capacities. For example, the tiger’s capacities for hunting and swimming are the foundation of its survival and reproduction. However, we must examine this view with rigor and avoid misunderstanding. Kant does not mean that if an individual fails to achieve their natural capacities, they lose their value as an individual or as a human being. Such an understanding is obviously too narrow and contrary to Kant’s original meaning.

The second proposition, regarding Kant’s exposition of the purpose of species, he explicitly refutes the previous view and clearly points out that every species has its unique purpose, but this does not mean every individual must realize this purpose. He further clarifies that the purpose of the human species lies in the full development of reason and enlightenment. We should take the development of reason as our task, but Kant’s view does not require everyone to reach the utmost of reason. For example, those individuals who cannot develop reason—such as persons with disabilities—do not thereby lose their qualification to be human. Kant emphasizes that, on the whole, as the human species, our goal is to promote the development of reason. This view profoundly embodies the core thought of Kant as an Enlightenment thinker and provides an important clue for our understanding of his thought.

The third proposition: we human beings, as a species, carry a clear mission, and we must unswervingly advance toward this mission. The core of this mission lies in enlightening wisdom and deepening our rational thinking. This process is by no means easy, pleasant, or smooth. Kant has a profound understanding of this and does not harbor naive fantasies.

The fourth proposition: regarding the goal of human development as a species, its process is essentially a struggle, a conflict, and a natural and unlimited pursuit of power. This process obviously carries significant hostility and antagonism. In this proposition, Kant proposes the famous concept of “unsocial sociability,” a concept of far-reaching influence that I deeply endorse and that demonstrates Kant’s extremely keen observation. This observation also fully proves that Kant is not a mere idealist; his understanding of human nature actually shares much in common with realists such as Hobbes and Machiavelli.

Kant points out that human progress originates from a unique attribute—namely, that unsociality which seems contrary to sociality. We ponder deeply why humans choose to form states and communities of shared destiny, and why they are willing to work together and organize various associations. Kant emphasizes that this does not stem from friendliness and trust in our nature; in fact, human nature at its essential level exhibits a certain unsociality. We may be selfish toward others, harbor hostility, and pursue the expansion of power and material abundance. However, it is precisely this unsocial attribute that in fact breeds a profound sociality. The selfish factors in this unsocial tendency make us realize that we cannot achieve grand goals alone; only by joining hands in cooperation—even with partners we may inwardly wish to exclude—can we attain our shared vision.

In discussing the fifth proposition, Kant elaborates in depth on the view of unsocial sociability he proposed earlier. He points out that although antisocial tendencies at the individual level may appear negative, when observed at the species and social levels, such tendencies actually contribute to the birth of many brilliant achievements of humanity. The splendid progress of human society is precisely based on certain “grinding” traits exhibited at the individual level. At the individual level, these traits may present a certain degree of conflict and challenge; yet it is precisely these conflicts and challenges that build the solid foundation of human culture, art, and social order.

These social and cultural arts possessing an unsocial character actually constitute a key component of our social order, and their value cannot be ignored. From the macro perspective of social and species development, their positive effects are obvious. However, at the micro individual level, when we observe with a deeper and more detailed perspective, these traits may be regarded as certain qualities that do not quite conform to mainstream values. Therefore, in comprehensively evaluating these cultural arts, we need to adopt a prudent attitude, affirming their social significance while also attending to their potential impact on individuals.

In deeply discussing the sixth proposition, we must examine with an extremely serious attitude the profound theoretical value it contains. In this proposition, Kant profoundly reveals that human nature is like crooked timber, not straight and flawless. We must clearly recognize that we are not wholly upright and unbiased, but possess many deficiencies.

He further analyzes and points out that human nature has inherent limitations; we humans do indeed face the risk of abusing freedom, which is an undeniable weak point in human nature. In view of this, we must squarely face this objective fact with a serious attitude and candidly admit that human beings may, driven by private desires, excessively exercise freedom and thereby cause adverse effects. To ensure social harmony, stability, justice, and fairness, we urgently need to construct a comprehensive legal system aimed at regulating and reasonably constraining the behavioral freedom of all citizens.

Regarding the seventh proposition, he profoundly points out that the state faces the potential risk of abusing free power; therefore, we urgently need to move onto the track of the rule of law to ensure the normative operation of power. Our ultimate goal is not limited to the harmonious coexistence of species, but is devoted to achieving the lofty goal of peace—this is the most sacred mission nature has bestowed on our species. However, it must be emphasized that Kant’s view here does not aim to shape morally perfect human beings, nor does it require every individual or state pursuing this goal to possess lofty moral stature. In fact, Kant profoundly recognizes the limitations of human nature and the risk that the state may abuse its free power. Only through deep exploration and understanding can we accurately grasp the core idea of Kant’s vision of perpetual peace.

In sum, Kant does not advocate establishing perpetual peace immediately or instantly as humanity’s sole pursuit, nor does he explicitly designate a particular state or individual as bearing a specific responsibility or obligation to realize perpetual peace. Likewise, he does not impose mandatory specific moral attitudes on individuals. On the contrary, Kant profoundly reveals that human nature contains inherent distortions and deficiencies; we are born with a certain degree of unsociality and aggressiveness, tending toward mutual opposition and conflict. He perceives that in the intercourse of states there often exist mutual calculation and aggression, seeking unlimited expansion and growth of power. He explicitly states that he is not a utopian theorist; he agrees with Hobbes’s view that human nature harbors mutual calculation and the desire for power expansion, and he also profoundly understands what Machiavelli emphasizes in international relations—namely, that princes should not be bound by morality in decision-making but must make non-moral choices between moral and non-moral options.

Yet while squarely facing the limitations of human nature, Kant profoundly guides us to deeply analyze the evolutionary trajectory of human history, thereby revealing a direction that transcends the individual level. This direction clearly points toward the continuous development of reason, civilization, enlightenment, and order, and its ultimate destination is the lofty goal of realizing perpetual peace.

Kant profoundly perceives that although human nature has inherent defects and limitations, this cannot become an obstacle to our pursuit of lofty moral realms and social ideals. He firmly believes that, under the guidance of reason, humanity has the capacity to gradually overcome its own deficiencies and drive society toward the grand goal of harmony and progress.

He clearly points out that this direction of development transcending individual limitations is the inevitable path of human civilizational progress. In this historical process, every individual should actively assume responsibility, fully play their role, and contribute their own strength to building a just, fair, harmonious, and stable social order, jointly advancing the development of human civilization.

At the same time, the state, as the core constituent element of society, shoulders the sacred mission of promoting continuous social progress. We must, through carefully formulating and strictly enforcing just laws and policies, create favorable conditions for humanity’s peace and development, ensuring social harmony and stability and that the people live and work in contentment.

Kant once profoundly pointed out that the achievement of perpetual peace is the common responsibility of the whole of society. The realization of this goal requires not only continuous self-improvement by individuals at the moral level but also profound reform and innovation by the state in multiple dimensions—political architecture, economic system, and cultural sphere. Only thus can social injustice and contradictions be gradually eliminated, moving toward a path of true peace and prosperity. In Kant’s conception, the road to lasting peace is indeed full of challenges and obstacles; yet as long as we unswervingly follow the principles of reason, justice, and righteousness, and persistently pursue progress and perfection, we will ultimately realize this lofty and great goal.

Kant forcefully rebuts those who claim that international relations cannot embody moral principles. He clearly states that although he agrees with the other’s analysis of human nature and depiction of evil, Kant emphasizes that he stands on a more macro and profound level in examining the issue. Specifically, even if the other stands on the realistic level, Kant also indicates that the level on which he stands not only acknowledges and understands the reality described by the other but further transcends this level, reaching a loftier and broader vision that reveals the core direction of historical development. This direction is the great journey toward lasting peace. On the stage of international politics, order should be its ultimate pursuit, not endless chaos. This is precisely Kant’s profound insight into international relations.

Kant displays an optimistic attitude; he is a firm realist. On the basis of profoundly recognizing the complexity and diversity of human nature, he insists that we should still hold an optimistic outlook on the direction of historical development. This attitude embodies his profound wisdom and positive expectations for social development. I firmly believe that this optimism is not groundless, nor is it blind optimism, still less a fantasy based on the ivory-tower moralist’s imagination. It is built on a solid realistic foundation and is the result of careful thought and scientific analysis.

He firmly believes that perpetual peace is a goal we should unswervingly pursue. However, this pursuit is not based on blind optimism about the feasibility of a world government, nor on the belief that world peace is an urgent task of the present moment. In the evolution of human society, this goal still deserves our sustained hope. We do not attribute this to a naive interpretation of human nature on Kant’s part; rather, it is because he profoundly perceives the complex sides of human nature—namely, that human nature contains selfishness, baseness, aggressiveness, and also greed. However, this does not mean we should abandon the pursuit of a better future; rather, we should more firmly believe and strive to overcome the weaknesses of human nature and jointly move toward a more harmonious and just society.

Based on this profound understanding, the selfish tendencies in human nature become, to some extent, the cornerstone driving the human species toward peace and prosperity.

In discussing the historical process, Kant points out that order often arises from conflict, peace often arises from the ordeal of war, and the formation of the public good is inseparable from the overcoming of private vices. This constitutes the core starting point of Kant’s political philosophy. In Kant’s expectation for human peace, he does not deny the baseness and evil of human nature; rather, he believes that these weaknesses at the individual level can ultimately lead to prosperity and peace in the evolution of the species.

Kant proposes that politicians need to understand deeply the baseness and evil in human nature and make good use of them. As a political philosopher, he stands above politicians and possesses a broader vision. From a macro and historical perspective, Kant observes that the reason human society can continuously progress is precisely that we constantly engage in conflict and struggle. This conflict is not disorderly, purposeless, or without end; rather, it is based on certain social goals and value orientations, aimed at driving society toward greater justice and harmony. The continuous development of human society and the frequent wars and conflicts in the long river of history are not accidental. These endless disputes stem from our deep yearning for peace and unremitting pursuit of it. It is precisely because we yearn for peace and are committed to building a more civilized social order that numerous conflicts and transformations have emerged in history. This yearning for peace is precisely an important driving force propelling humanity’s continuous progress and development.

Regarding the length and depth of this essay, it is necessary for us to explore its connotation in depth. I wish to rebut a previous way of understanding. Kant, although regarded as an optimist, is more accurately an optimistic realist. After perceiving the complexity and multifaceted nature of human nature, he still insists on maintaining optimism about the direction of historical development. Although I personally may not reach his level of optimism, I deeply respect his optimistic attitude.

Kant’s optimism is not built on fantasy or blind moralism. He firmly believes that perpetual peace is a goal human society can strive for, but this is not based on the possibility of a world government or the immediate realization of world peace, but on human society’s sustained hope for this goal. He holds this view not because of a naive fantasy about human nature, but because of a profound insight into human nature’s selfishness, baseness, aggressiveness, and greed. It is precisely on this basis that he believes peace and prosperity for humanity as a species can be realized.

Kant’s political philosophy takes as its starting point “order from conflict, peace from war, and the public good from private vices.” He does not deny the baseness and evil of human nature; rather, he believes that individual evil at the species level will lead to prosperity and peace. Politicians need to utilize the baseness and evil in human nature, while a political philosopher like Kant stands at a higher level, perceiving the essence of human society advancing through conflict in the historical process.

Kant believes that the endless conflicts in history do not stem from disorder or blindness, but from humanity’s yearning for peace and a more civilized order. Only on such a basis can we deeply understand Kant’s hope for perpetual peace and its foundation. Through in-depth study of Kant, I have developed two views quite different from the general public. First, Kant is not as obscure and difficult to understand as the public believes; although his works may appear profound, they actually express his academic thought in a concise, clear, and straightforward manner. Second, Kant is not an idealist or an armchair moralist; he is a philosopher who remains optimistic on the basis of realistic premises. This optimism commands my deep respect.
