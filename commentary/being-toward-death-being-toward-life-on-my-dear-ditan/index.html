Being-toward-Death, Being-toward-Life — On My Dear Ditan

Abstract — Life is first a tragic existence. This is an objective fact of life itself. Humans, as the subjects of life, do not control its direction. Birth is not a choice; death is inevitable.

Ann Rongze MaoCommentary
On this pageFull textPublished version ↗

Life is first a tragic existence.

This is an objective fact of life itself.

Humans, as the subjects of life, do not control its direction. Birth is not a choice; death is inevitable. “One’s birth is no longer a matter for debate, but a fact handed over by God.” When life is bestowed, the ending is simultaneously signed—“Death is a festival that must arrive.” In this sense, life carries its end from the very beginning.

Yet if one stays only at this level, the emphasis on tragedy becomes hollow.

Tragedy is tragedy precisely because it is unavoidable yet must be borne.

Misfortune is the normal structure of life. The difference lies only in how, and with what intensity, it falls on a particular person. Subjective effort matters, but never eliminates chance, suffering, or impermanence. One cannot escape that fundamental powerlessness.

But if life were only this, it wouldn’t be worth writing about again and again.

I acknowledge life’s objective tragedy, but merely emphasizing tragedy itself is meaningless. “We are but mayflies between heaven and earth, a single grain in the vast ocean.” It is precisely this smallness that forces us to ask—what is the meaning of being human? What is value?

Once the question is raised, life’s tragedy is no longer just fate’s cruelty, but a question at the level of existence: if everything will eventually disappear, is meaning only an illusion? If death swallows everything, is life ultimately void?

This is the deeper layer of tragic consciousness—falling into nothingness is extremely dangerous, and a modern anxiety difficult to avoid.

Yet, in this inescapable awareness of tragedy, the dignity of life is awakened. For tragedy exists precisely because it cannot be changed; and humans, in not being able to change it, still insist on thinking, feeling, and choosing.

Simply perceiving tragedy cannot save life. But without a clear awareness of tragedy, one cannot move toward a genuine affirmation of life.

Despair is not the end—it is the starting point of all true affirmation.

People live simply for the sake of living, not for anything beyond living. Yu Hua’s Fugui crystallizes this in the most extreme way: living itself is the entire reason. When one stops demanding extra meaning from fate, the threat of death reconciles with life to some extent.

So what distinguishes humans from other living beings?

The answer may lie in the question itself.

Only humans, in the finite world, engage in infinite contemplation.

It is on this point that Shi Tiesheng’s writing shows its irreplaceable significance. His work is inseparable from his life circumstances. A smooth, comfortable life rarely forces one to face existence’s basebone. But when the body is struck by fate, one is pushed to the edge of a cliff and must look down.

This “sense of standing at the abyss” forces one to penetrate the cover of daily life and confront the essential structure of existence. Shi’s presentation is precisely the breadth and depth of spirit reached at that limit.

In “Talking to Myself,” Shi identified three fundamental predicaments: loneliness, desire, and death. But loneliness and death become predicaments precisely because of desire—the desire for perfect communication breeds loneliness; the desire for immortality breeds fear.

Buddhism thus sees desire as the source of suffering. But Shi did not deny desire. On the contrary, he always defended its rationality. In his thought, life’s tragedy comes not from desire itself, but from the clear gaze upon life.

After encountering impermanence, recognizing cruelty, still striving to break through—this is the true starting point of Shi’s life philosophy.

The search for life’s meaning is the basic drive for human survival. Once meaning collapses, one cannot continue. Death is the greatest threat to meaning—it makes all efforts seem to point toward nothingness.

So how does one continue to live in the face of certain death?

Unlike many Western philosophies‘ skepticism or denial of eternity, Shi grounds life’s meaning in “eternity.” He argues: meaning is not only epistemological but ontological. It is not that eternity “gives” meaning to life, but that the individual enters eternity through authentic experience of meaning.

This eternity is qualitative, not quantitative; spiritual openness is not the same as infinite extension.

On the surface, this seems to weaken individual subjectivity. But deeper, it is the freest choice—the individual voluntarily places the self at a higher spiritual center. Redemption comes not from rational deduction, but from existential realization.

Shi’s equanimity toward life and death is the best proof of this thought’s validity.

Thus, the eternity he seeks is not attachment to physical life, but a demand for “quality” in spiritual life. It is a posture of selflessness—not escape, but composure after facing death. It is a truly heroic spirit.

In My Dear Ditan, Shi Tiesheng writes: “It seemed this ancient garden had been waiting for me, enduring four hundred years of vicissitudes.” This is a metaphor of fate, and a response to existence.

Ditan is like the earth itself—accepting nourishment, accepting waste; bearing all, yet always returning life. If Ditan was fate’s gift to Shi, enabling him to achieve self-redemption in disability and suffering, then My Dear Ditan itself is Shi’s gift to the world.

Perhaps his rational exploration was never completed. But precisely because it’s unfinished, thought remains open. With persistent and restrained rationality, he guides us into genuine reflection on life—this, perhaps, is the greatest meaning of his words to us.

May 2025. Revised.

Life, an Eternal Dance.

Shi Tiesheng wrote in illness: “The universe, with its unceasing desire, refines a dance into eternity.”

To be human is to have desire—curiosity, creation, the impulse to understand. Through “participation” and “shaping,” humans make the world manifest meaning.

Being-toward-death means living more clearly.

If desire is eternal, is pain also part of this dance?

Of course.

Because desire itself is a gap.

Between reality and ideals lies a gap—a structural pain. Wanting to understand means not yet understanding. Wanting to arrive means not yet arrived.

Buddhism speaks of “conditioned arising and emptiness of nature”: existence is process; the identities, health, status one clings to are destined to change and dissipate. For this reason, pain is a concomitant of life’s operation. This is not nihilism.

Pain is the harmony of the dance.

We cannot make it disappear, but we can learn to listen to it, understand it, coexist with it.

Humans are both dancers and spectators—able to participate fully, yet in a moment, quietly see the whole performance’s grandeur and pathos.

That moment is reconciliation with life.

Death is never great—life is.

Life is greatness—the echo of creation’s first breath. It is smallness—the tremor of a falling grain of dust.

Thus, with eternal force one lifts the weight of dust; with dust’s lightness one probes eternity’s depth.

Death is not a glorious finale, but a departure stripped of all pretense.

Life’s tranquility interprets itself in a peaceful death.

And before that—

Living itself is the greatest thing.
