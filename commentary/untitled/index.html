In modern society, the standardized template of so-called “success” and “happiness”—elaborate and widespread—clearly marks the “correct” path: study, establish a career, start a family, accumulate wealth, and finally reach a destination called “retiring in peace.” Not wrong, to some extent efficient. But most are accustomed to following the map, because it eliminates the fear of wandering alone in the wilderness.

The ineffable “happiness” is translated into a narrative about “having” and “showing.” Where there is desire, there is consumption. Capital is pervasive, making us believe satisfaction lies in the next purchase or identity upgrade. Social recognition, material comfort, and a sense of being “on the right track” reward the compliant. One could say it’s stable and effective—at least letting the masses temporarily forget metaphysical questions.

However, this theory’s legitimacy has a necessary premise: assuming a long and predictable future. The core logic is “delayed gratification”—sacrifice now for future happiness.

This premise is fragile. When awareness of life’s finitude—serious illness, accident—shines into life, the theory’s foundations begin to crumble. Once the promised “happiness destination” disappears, “rule-followers” like pious pilgrims reaching the end discover the temple was a mirage. All sacrifices for the future become zero before the realization that “the present may be the end.”

Death’s approach exposes the virtual setting based on infinite time, and the transaction that may never be completed.

If not for an illusory endpoint, what should we live for? The authority to define meaning is returned to the internal self.

This leads to the Sisyphus logic: “success” lies not in pushing the boulder to a fictional peak, but in whether, during the push, one feels the stone’s roughness, muscles‘ tension, sweat’s fall, even appreciating the sunset’s glow in exhaustion. The secular “quantity” of accumulation—wealth, reputation—is part of the consideration; but more important is “quality” depth—the thickness of experience, the thickness of life. The courage to still choose engagement after recognizing absurdity.

So why do people, while perceiving absurdity, still follow the template?

Human instinct seeks security, belonging, certainty. The template provides the path of least resistance, relieving the heavy burden of navigating the wilderness alone.

Within a stable framework, following the template is a rational choice to satisfy basic needs and maximize personal welfare. The system’s immense inertia—education, family, financial networks—makes deviation extremely costly. Thus, most people’s “indulgence” resembles pragmatic survival wisdom.

This wisdom can be understood through a dichotomy of two subject types:

There seem to be “clear-eyed sufferers” and “ignorant happy ones.”

The former see through the standardized solution’s falsity, gaining spiritual liberation and choice, but must bear the pain of friction with rules. The latter may not deeply question meaning; they walk the map’s path, using its stability, belonging, and material comfort to effectively counter life’s inherent anxiety.

Mocking “ignorant happiness” may underestimate the template’s powerful buffering effect, while praising “clear-eyed pain” may overlook the real pressures on the body in the wilderness. Most people’s compliance is a trade-off between spiritual examination and security.

The simplest, entertaining example: from Camus’s The Stranger to Peter Weir’s The Truman Show to the contemporary digital society—the technology, efficiency, and concealment of producing “outsiders” have changed. Textually, Truman is a successful product of power discipline—his worldview carefully designed, coherent, knowable; his life has a storyline, goals, actions still connected to external rules. Meursault is a failed product of discipline—having not been implanted with any stable worldview, his life refuses script, wandering outside systems, resisting in silence.

In contemporary society, “outsiders” are efficiently produced in new, more concealed forms: e.g., disconnected from digital ecology, algorithm-unpredictable, discourse-insulated. Common feature: cannot be incorporated into the system’s meaning production. Meanwhile, accepted forms of resistance have shifted from Truman’s “wall-breaking” to Meursault’s “non-cooperation.” The former destroys the original system; the latter, through refusing false performance and refusing to supplement meaning in meaninglessness, lets the system idle on the individual, creating long-term structural friction.

Discipline is everywhere, but freedom’s essence is internal.

The mass-defined standard answer may provide security, but when the shadow of life’s finiteness appears, clearly and passionately charting one’s own trajectory becomes the individual’s optimal solution—in every moment of pushing the boulder, in every silence refusing discipline.

As Camus said, one must imagine Sisyphus happy—clearly recognizing fate, and actively choosing one’s way of being in the midst of absurdity.

The thickness of life lies in confronting absurdity, bearing it, and creating one’s own freedom within it.
