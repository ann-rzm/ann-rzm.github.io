Same World, Different Filters

People are different.

A common misconception is that these differences are mainly personality traits or chance experiences. They actually stem from how individuals understand the world is operating, and what roles they are allowed to play in it.

We usually explain differences with “personality, education, experience”—but these are more results, external manifestations after long-term operation of deeper cognitive structures. The inner shaper of this form can be defined as worldview—a relatively stable belief structure about the world’s operating logic and one’s position within it.

It determines: reality, possibility, futility, “naturalness.” Not directly directing “should do,” but based on facts, “who am I,” “how should I choose.”

Thus, worldview differences have nothing to do with morality—they can be seen as different survival strategy differentiations.

I. Materialism and Idealism

As analytical tools, we can roughly distinguish between materialist and idealist ideal models. It’s important to emphasize: these are just working modes of explaining the world.

The materialist orientation understands the world as a system governed by objective laws. Resources are limited, competition real, causality stable. It draws boundaries for individuals, telling them what cannot be crossed, what cannot be avoided; revealing constraints.

The idealist orientation emphasizes meaning. It does not deny material conditions, but holds that the direction of action, the reason for endurance, the legitimacy of sacrifice come from consciousness’s interpretation of reality.

In reality, individuals almost never fully belong to either extreme. But the difference lies in: when the two conflict, which explanation does one prioritize? (E.g., in failure, do you see structure or fate? In success, luck or self-worth?)

Different explanatory templates.

II. From Cognition to Production

Worldviews are built in the mind, constantly verified through behavior, and in turn reinforced by social structures.

The most primitive driver of human behavior is indeed survival and reproduction—material fact. But consciousness narratively packages instinct, making it seem reasonable, justified, even noble. Thus, behavior is given meaning; action. Again and again, when meaning is repeatedly confirmed in groups, it rises to institutions, norms, class structures.

From this perspective, class is the solidified form of different worldviews under material conditions.

Whoever controls resources has priority in explaining reality; and the power to explain reality ultimately determines who is seen as “normal,” “successful,” “valuable.”

This raises a question: how does society maintain overall stability amid objective conflicts of interest?

—Through ideology, a unified interpretive framework.

For most people, this is subjectively voluntary—they actively choose the explanation version with the lowest personal cost in understanding the world.

III. Survival Competition

From an evolutionary perspective, consciousness was originally a tool for more efficient environmental prediction and coordinated action. But in complex societies, consciousness began producing abstract objects beyond immediate survival needs—“people love gods”—currency, honor, faith, identity.

Essentially tools, but gradually treated as ends.

When individuals begin living for capital, prestige, or ideas, these do increase survival probability in competitive environments. But when tools take center stage in value judgment, individuals are re-instrumentalized.

Who is the subject? Who is used?

IV. Freedom Is Visibility

In such structures, talking about “freedom” easily slides into self-consolation. Freedom is visibility of how rules operate. Most people’s so-called “choices” happen among already filtered options. Paths, rhythms, goals are often preset by environment before the individual forms a stable self.

Thus, freedom is a high-cognitive-cost, high-psychological-cost capability. It means: being aware which desires don’t originate from oneself; distinguishing tools from ends; when necessary, bearing the risk of “not being explained” and “not being identified.”

This is also why most people don’t need to pursue this “freedom.” A stable explanation of the world is more survival-rational than constantly dismantling it.

Differences are rational responses to different survival costs under the same structure. Some choose integration, some choose friction; some need meaning to act, some can only survive in honesty. Reality doesn’t favor any subject—it only needs predictability. Individual differences are just traces left by long-term operation that cannot be completely smoothed.
