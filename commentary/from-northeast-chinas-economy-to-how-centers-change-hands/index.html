From Northeast China’s Economy to How “Centers” Change Hands

Through ancient rivers and mountains ten thousand li of splendor, boundless scenery, snowline stretching.

Limited IP: Heilongjiang. The vast plains beyond the high-speed rail rush backward. Geographic displacement often brings a certain macroscopic detachment. Northeast China’s situation inevitably evokes America’s Rust Belt (Ohio) or Russia’s Ural Mountains.

Why do traditional industrial regions generally lag behind new regions? What is “rust”? Industrial-era logic similarly, AI-era similar?

For social organizations to survive, output must exceed input. Output depends on two variables: what is used, and how. One is material foundation—e.g., coal, computing power. Two is transformation capability—e.g., steam engine for coal, large model for graphics cards.

“Productivity determines relations of production; economic base determines superstructure.” “Explosive growth” qualitatively transforms transformation capability; thus old structures are no longer optimal.

Costs are mainly two categories: explicit and implicit. Regarding the former, raw materials are one aspect, but operating cost I1 takes the larger share. More people need management; more processes need assembly lines.

The biggest resistance to transformation is the hidden switching cost I2. E.g., a city trained 100,000 steelworkers; but if industry shifts to precision manufacturing or digital economy, you can’t make 100,000 people become engineers overnight. Assets can become liabilities “in an instant.”

Living space is net surplus, Net Value, NV. If NV > 0, there’s hope; if NV < 0, income can’t cover expenses; if NV is the global maximum, this place is the “center.”

History as mirror.

(This section’s thinking was inspired by Guns, Germs, and Steel, etc.)

In the 15th century, Venice wasn’t the strongest city-state. But the arsenal’s assembly-line production reduced operating cost I1 to the extreme. Geographical endowment inferior, but transformation efficiency high.

In the 16th century, the Age of Discovery. Da Gama rounded the Cape of Good Hope, opening the route from the East to Lisbon. Venetians weren’t unaware of the crisis, but switching cost I2 was too high. They considered the Suez Canal but were technologically limited; considered ocean-going ships but were risk-averse. They could only bet on familiar Mediterranean trade.

The new route lowered operating costs to a level Venice couldn’t reach—a dimensionality reduction attack. As for Venice’s slow death: better to accept certain shrinkage than, or rather be unable to, pay the price of embracing the unknown.

The Netherlands had no traditional resources, its land even below sea level. But the Dutch proposed standardized cargo ships, joint-stock company systems, modern banking—greatly reducing trust costs.

Britain’s logic was similar. Besides coal and iron, the Royal Navy lowered global shipping security costs (for its own).

In the 20th century, the center moved to New Amsterdam. Pittsburgh’s steel mills weren’t inefficient, but Wall Street’s financing efficiency was higher. In an era of exponential technological iteration, lower-cost capital acquisition and faster trial and error had higher priority.

What is rust? Or “why do traditional industrial regions decline”? Not that the optimal solution can’t occur in the original place; but as constraints iterate rapidly, if the original place doesn’t sync, latecomers easily surpass. “Rust belts” can be simplified as switching cost I2 exceeding the social willingness to pay NV—but this is a byproduct of iterative optimization.

Change involves legitimacy—getting people to accept the distribution rules as just. State-owned workers‘ identity was a kind of legitimacy. They burned for the country, but the country needed a new fuel.

Workers could be laid off, but workers don’t forget being workers. You can’t make someone proud of blast furnaces suddenly think delivering on a scooter is also a proper job.

(*But all labor is proper work.)

History is filled with lives, not just formulas; structure grows in bone and blood. If you’ve lived your whole life in a compound, receiving state targets, eating collective meals—this becomes identity, rules that give dignity.

Social being determines social consciousness, but social consciousness has lag. Used to asking the plan for targets, not necessarily understanding how to ask the market for premium. Cognition is the biggest rust—harder to change than physical facilities.

So why does history repeat? The world constantly changes constraints for the future—“history’s wheel rolls forward”—but structures can only optimize for the past. Referring to Schumpeter’s innovation theory: creating new structures necessarily destroys old ones. Order is continuously replaced; the efficient expands, the inefficient contracts. Spiraling ascent always involves breaking old forms.

Industrial-era sunk costs were blast furnaces, railways. AI-era similarly, but no longer physical.

Path assumptions require looking left, right, forward. Right: relying on external (state transfer payments). Left: fundamentally changing education and research structures—like genetic mutation. Nodes: e.g., clean energy or agricultural technology—irreplaceable links in the supply chain.

Haven’t heard firecrackers in a long time. The roar is an inside-out explosion of fire breaking free from the core. The crackling bustle, the sharpness scraping the air into sparks.

Rust is oxidation, not rot. Iron is still there; the bonding has just changed.

Long sky, clouds gather, fire and smoke—changed the world.
