From the Relationship of “Dao” and “De” to Contemporary Employment Structure

Abstract — Employment difficulties—whose difficulty? Manifesting in youth, but roots not limited to age. Structural adjustment and evaluation systems have time and temperature gaps.

Ann Rongze MaoCommentary
On this pageFull textDaoDeBreaking and EstablishingEmploymentPublished version ↗

Employment difficulties—whose difficulty? Manifesting in youth, but roots not limited to age. Structural adjustment and evaluation systems have time and temperature gaps. The contradiction between digitized advanced productivity and industrial-era old relations of production—this is not simply a “generational issue.” Western theories can explain part, but the fundamental issue lies in analytical frameworks that fail or become alienated in change—the operation of historical process. This essay uses the Dao De Jing as an analytical reference tool to explore employment and industrial transformation. Views are only records of immature personal thinking, not targeting any specific individual or organization.

Dao

“Dao” is the objective historical process, the sum of all objective laws. This is a dynamic, never-ceasing process of generation and dissolution. Specifically, civilization operates in long-term trends—prosperity, decline, renewal, unfolding cyclically. Extreme development inevitably triggers regression and transformation. In the labor market, historical currents are a continuous process of reconstructing rules themselves, manifesting as unpredictable violent fluctuations. Under high-frequency technological impact, any capability built on stable expectations may rapidly depreciate. Individuals, or groups, can follow the momentum or ride the trend. Historical processes favor no one—including civilizations, institutions, individuals. It only screens and eliminates according to its own logic. Today’s “Dao” is shifting from reliance on tacit experience to explicit data flows. Material constraints lie in the assetization of data factors. Take Black Lake Technology as an example: traditional manual recorded experience is “dead asset,” while real-time cloud-generated production data is “living credit.” Material constraints are the foundation of all civilizational construction. Resources, technology, demographics, ecological carrying capacity—they serve as the base. Further, driving the “Dao” forward requires motivation—contradiction. “Being and non-being give rise to each other.” Creation and destruction, establishment and dissolution, technology and labor, new capabilities and old evaluation systems. The internal movement of history involves yin-yang harmony. The unity of opposites‘ conflict serves as an engine for evolution. Thus, history’s operation is a cyclical, spiraling long-term path. Straight-line progress isn’t necessarily good, but adaptability to turning points is certainly scarce. The 18th-century Industrial Revolution: handicraft replaced by machines; human labor roles transformed dramatically. Broadly, “useful” and “useless” are redefined through technological innovation, restructuring social relations. Now similarly, with AI and automation, the “being” of much programmatic labor is losing value, while the “use” of seemingly “non-being” areas like creativity is expanding sharply.

De

“De” essentially is the adaptive capacity an individual or civilization obtains from “Dao.” It can be approximated as the system’s pricing power over individual value. True “De” does not claim to be “De,” thus aligns with “Dao.” The lower kind only preserves “De’s” form, fearing loss of external norms. About the containment relationship of Dao, De, Ren, Yi, Li. A civilization, from depending on inner vitality to operating naturally, to increasingly depending on external, rigid rules to maintain order. Each “loss” marks a step further from its spontaneous source; thus one must resort to more complex, more coercive artificial norms to compensate. Thus, “De” is not moral preaching or judgment of good and evil, but order, norms, and stabilization mechanisms—used to compensate for disorder after Dao retreats. Translated into reality: rules, evaluations, seniority, thresholds. From mechanism design theory, institutions operate on the premise of not trusting individuals. Their function is limited constraint on rational individuals and self-interested behavior. They don’t directly create value but ensure the system doesn’t collapse from excess freedom. Therefore, “self-generated De” is real capability formed according to current historical conditions. “Alienated De” solidifies past successful experience into untouchable dogma for vested interests. When “Dao” has changed, it loses the ability to continue “getting.” About the consequences of this alienation: “Being benefits; non-being creates use.” If the system excessively pursues efficiency and quantifiable metrics (“being”), compressing innovation capability and diversity (“non-being”), it becomes fragile under technological impact. For example, excessive path standardization reduces labor market resilience. The past was “pawnshop thinking”—looking at stock collateral. The future may lean toward dynamic cash flow—real-time problem-solving capability. But live flow is hard to quantify, so most flood the “stock metrics” competitive field. Eastern and Western path differences are survival strategies arising from different constraints, each historically rational. “Dao” has no East or West, but in responding to “Dao’s” constraints, civilizations develop different survival strategies—“De”—due to initial conditions and real choices. Claims of “superiority” are fragile; the only standard: can the civilization’s organizational form (“De”) maintain vitality under its material constraints (“Dao”) over the long term, without turn “things must decay” self-destruction?

The US employment system is highly mobile. “De’s” advantage lies in elasticity—“softness overcomes hardness.” High mobility brings insecurity but enables faster workforce reorganization to respond to technological changes. But without social security, this “softness” may slide into social tearing. Some orders played significant stability functions under specific historical conditions. But when “Dao” shifts toward higher mobility and innovation demand, their rigidity weakens elasticity to some extent. Any successful “De” naturally risks formalization and hollowing. Laozi’s view: a healthy system must retain the quality of “wu wei” (non-action). But “wu wei” is not “hands-off”—it is organized, measured “action” under deep understanding of historical laws. “De’s” final legitimacy can only be judged by “Dao.”

Breaking and Establishing

When “alienated De” seriously hinders “Dao’s” progress, “breaking” unfolds through internal contradictions. “Reversal” is “Dao’s” fundamental movement. “Things must decay”—what doesn’t align with continuous evolution will inevitably decline early. Thus, “breaking” is “Dao’s” inevitable operating logic—a historical-level mean reversion to imbalance. “The softest thing in the world rides over the hardest.” “Establishing” is not forceful acquisition, but assisting new things according to their internal laws, clearing space for them to grow naturally. Take Black Lake Technology again: initially a tool for cost reduction and efficiency, evolving into a linking platform, a benchmark for the industry ecosystem. Parts on the assembly line are dependent tools; those who can solve problems are ecological nodes. Similarly, today’s industrial and employment structures are undergoing a similar “break-establish” process: old positions and certain dogmas are being eliminated; new capabilities and diversified positions need space to survive.

Employment

Returning to employment and industrial transformation: industrial-era logic can’t answer digital-era reality. Summation can’t match exponential growth. The industrial era was “human + machine”—humans were necessary. Now the digital era—algorithms replace humans to some extent. Productivity has undergone qualitative change. Carbon-based labor meets silicon-based productivity. Traditional skills and positions are being redefined. Lack of chips means lack of premium. Why anxiety? Because logical points are broken.

Education → market labor: Regarding the former, public schools were largely designed for factory assembly lines and civil service selection—favoring obedient, rule-following, standardized talent. Regarding the latter, society’s widely recognized positions still lean toward stability and path clarity. They appear matching, but actually positions increasingly require “independent, flexible, composite” capabilities. This is the failure of “dependent employment.” Any position that cannot independently solve problems outside an organization is dependent. Future workers are not long-term supported roles, but economic ecological nodes with problem-solving capability—talent that can autonomously survive and iterate in uncertainty. Security comes from multiple action choices and flexible adjustment capability.

Individual pain, confusion are never unique. When countless individuals face similar difficulties, these “personal problems” coalesce into group predicaments.

History indeed has many “cracks,” but people don’t passively choose gaps. Human practice can lever structure, create new spaces and positions. “What can I contribute” vs. “What can history give me”? The latter is often painful—surrendering initiative. Pure capitalism is amoral—only profit. What is profit? Dedication, returns, information asymmetry. The Dao De Jing says fluctuation is normal, softness overcomes hardness, don’t over-intervene—how to respond rationally to uncertainty. In other words, “Dao” in capital is constraints. Understanding and participating in contradictions, rather than shouting “morality” hollowly, better helps individuals, collectives, and systems survive. Winds and waves, the tide washes away. The future belongs to new quality productivity; the fading of old shackles is irreversible. No one knows what the future holds—I don’t either. Macro is hot, micro is cold. What can be done: either expand the circle, or solidify the roots.

“The future is bright; the path is tortuous.” “Ten thousand years are too long—seize the day.”
