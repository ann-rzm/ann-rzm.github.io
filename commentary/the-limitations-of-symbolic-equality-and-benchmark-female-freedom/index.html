The Limitations of “Symbolic Equality” and Benchmark Female Freedom

Abstract — In contemporary Western developed countries, supporting gender equality has become mainstream consensus. Even with legal and institutional progress, the substance of women’s liberation remains debated. Acknowledging historical injustices and reform efforts does not mean all equality measures are effective.

In contemporary Western developed countries, supporting gender equality has become mainstream consensus. Even with legal and institutional progress, the substance of women’s liberation remains debated. Acknowledging historical injustices and reform efforts does not mean all equality measures are effective. Some paths, under the banner of equality, have to some extent solidified vested interests.

This discussion is primarily based on institutional practices and academic literature from Western developed countries, for theoretical analysis only.

In public discourse, “equality” often confuses three levels: equality of opportunity (fair starting point), equality of outcome (balanced endpoints), and equality of process (fair rules).

Short-term “most efficient” is often outcome equality—quotas, for example. However, outcome-oriented approaches highly depend on a premise: that the competitive field is already sufficiently neutral and safe—i.e., “approaching equal structures.” But in reality, the dominant structure has not been corrected; outcome equality presumes its unfulfilled premise. In relatively mature fields, this has indeed pressured structural change. But when variance is too large, simply imposing outcome equality easily transfers risks that should be borne by structural arrangements to disadvantaged individuals, leading to reverse discrimination controversies.

Conversely, Elizabeth Anderson’s “relational equality” emphasizes: inequality is actually manifested in relationships—whether one is dominated, humiliated, exposed to asymmetric risk. This cannot be solved by position alone.

If an arrangement’s starting point is only outcome equality without synchronously correcting internal incentive structures, punishment mechanisms, and power distribution—sheep among wolves.

Superficial showcase measures are low-cost, low-risk. As Olson’s collective action theory shows, institutions controlled by concentrated interests tend toward low-cost symbolic progress, avoiding touching core frameworks.

“Benchmark” individual breakthroughs have historical significance but limited impact on the overall group. After a Western country’s first female head of state took office, she brought no significant structural progress in gender equality or welfare policies for lower-class women—even adopting conservative positions.

Who chose her—whom does she represent? Have budgets, agendas, accountability structures changed? Has risk exposure for lower-class women decreased? Under the premise of success being permitted, risks are often borne by disadvantaged groups.

Equality that benefits the masses lies in escaping domination relationships and asymmetric risk exposure, not in individual positions or symbols.

Source: TIME Magazine, Person of the Year 2025 (“The Architects of AI”).

In reality, “successful” benchmark cases powerfully prove the original structure’s stubbornness. In TIME’s 2025 “Person of the Year” (The Architects of AI): among male leaders, AMD’s Lisa Su and AI’s Fei-Fei Li stand out. They’ve driven local improvements—AMD’s female engineer ratio increased, Fei-Fei Li advocates AI ethics and diversity. But this highly depends on exceptional individual capability and resource control—still a low-probability event.

—Is it only necessary for sufficiently powerful women to qualify for equality? If equality requires individual capability as prerequisite, is the legitimacy of “born equal” still valid? Equality is not the privilege of a few elites.

On the contrary. Equality is how institutions treat individuals, not what individuals must prove. It means not being systematically dominated, not exposed to higher risk due to gender, not deprived of life choices. Without these three, it’s merely symbolic equality.

Excellent cases don’t prove “women can too.” The problem is: “only extremely few women of this caliber can temporarily escape structural oppression.” Precisely because equality in reality is often limited to a few, it highlights the necessity of rule-based constraints. Again, equality is not a description of reality, but a norm for rules.

Even if reality is imperfect, we still uphold the ideal of equality and the macro vision of substantive equality. This is not a matter of moral obligation, but of happiness and institutional sustainability. As the capabilities approach states: “justice” lies in whether social institutions can guarantee everyone the substantive freedom to choose their life.

Symbolic progress is easy to achieve but cannot bear the responsibility of the overall structure. A purely efficient society, purely interest-driven, may have high short-term output; but lacking legitimacy and sustainability, order that cannot be recognized by the majority will eventually collapse.

Equality is not just the halo of benchmark figures, but everyone—whether elite or under—being able to autonomously decide their lives. The path to equality will inevitably be slow, difficult, full of friction. But structural change makes it possible to transform women from individual breakthroughs to a broader, more sustainable freedom.

Keep Marching.
