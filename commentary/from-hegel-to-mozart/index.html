From Hegel to Mozart

Why do people need entertainment? Music, for example.

The masses have needs, summarizable as: self-identity (who I am), emotional expression (what I feel), social belonging (where I belong), and physical pleasure (I feel good). Pop music is catchy because it arrives most efficiently—direct and intense—meeting the mass market’s needs for instant resonance, emotional outlet, and social currency.

Art has no high or low—that’s class product. But what withstands time, the “masters”—Mozart, Beethoven, Bach—also precisely serve contemporary listeners‘ needs. Music is indeed a medium for expressing ideas, but more a form for containing contradictions without resolving them.

Music, especially Western absolute music, is described as “not taking anything from the external world as material, but pure art itself.” (E.T.A. Hoffmann) So it’s itself a form of thought, an autonomous spirit. As Hegel describes in Aesthetics Lectures on Music: “Music, as the most romantic art, unfolds inner spiritual movement in pure time form, thereby affirming the subject’s freedom.”

Sounds pretentious. Meaning: people like different music essentially choosing resonance with a phase of their inner self. For recharging—some need fast-charging stress relief, some need long-term order, some need massive battery to fight fate.

E.g., love pop/rock: immediate emotional dialectic. Breakup—contradiction raised, intensified in the chorus, temporarily dissolved or indulged in repetition.

Why do people need music? Not to find meaning—that’s result, byproduct. The root can be understood as a safe place to dwell when meaning hasn’t yet been established. Language needs clear reference; philosophy needs logical closure. But often people neither know what happened nor are ready to conclude. This is music’s advantage—allowing one to remain in unexplained states.

What is music? Many things “can’t be said clearly, can’t be explained, can’t be resolved”—but exist, needing a legitimate container. Title “From Hegel to Mozart”—music and philosophy share the same root but different functions.

Philosophy is a race to answers; music is coexistence without end. Take Hegel’s dialectic: identify problem (thesis), investigate (antithesis), truth revealed (synthesis). Contradiction resolved or sublated. But as above, music knows tension exists, takes it as starting point, lets it coexist, finally suspends (displayed).

E.g., you’re broken up—hate and want simultaneously; self-denial yet can’t let go. This is the most real contradiction. Hegel (philosopher) would come and analyze: this relationship is thesis, your independence is antithesis, ultimately you’ll gain personality growth through pain—synthesis; giving a logical solution, rationally understanding.

But a great love song / a Mozart adagio—what does it do? Can’t give you a conclusion. But it uses a sweet yet painful melody, complex harmonic shifts, to present that bizarre entanglement, even beautified. Conclusion: “I am this complex mixture right now.” It gives contradiction dignity and form without eliminating. (Comparable to T vs F people =)

Take Bach, Mozart, Beethoven. Genius isn’t doing nothing and suddenly exploding at a turning point. People don’t first have style then create; they’re forced into a corner by reality, only able to create one optimal psychological structure. Music, or all expressive media, is just the natural externalization of that inner framework.

References: History of European Music (Zhang Hongdao, 1983)

Bach’s era: world’s rules were certain. Lutheran Germany: God, religion, truth. Thus the meaning of life is understanding order, obeying order, praising order. Plus the craft-based professional structure—artists in the same social class as carpenters, clockmakers—technicians. So delivering works per contract without expressing “I.”

In this situation, if you’re Bach, three paths: resist order or abandon depth—first is social suicide, second pipeline mediocrity. So he chose to perfect order, gaining resources within society. His security came from rules; value from structural integrity. Thus his music: emotions embedded in system—necessarily “steady,” multi-voiced parallel—because the world is multi-layered but unified.

Mozart’s era: old order still existed but was loosening. Nobility in power, but the Enlightenment had arrived; thus burghers (commoners) began consuming art. He was exhibited as a wonder from childhood—object-type appreciation, observation, use—naturally a floating sense of impending fall. Because the world conveys: needed (for talent) but no safety net.

How to satisfy nobles and the public without being swallowed? Thus near-perfect humanity in the cracks. One difference from Beethoven: in Mozart you hear a pleasure of being accepted—emotions through beautification and balance. Beethoven is almost wild—extreme, self, peak state.

Beethoven: French Revolution—turbulent times. Old world collapsing, new not formed, he faced deafness—near-despair. Thus only two choices: mental breakdown, or raising “self” to highest. Some call him solitary, paranoid. But if “I” isn’t the absolute center, he’d be swallowed—his environment was extreme conflict.

Musicians were forced to grow into different ways of living; music is the most honest voice of that way of living. Every choice is a deterministic solution under current cognitive conditions and comprehensive environment. Music preference is one’s way of handling “tension” in facing the world.

Most view: music and philosophy corresponding: Bach-Kant—“I think” echoing “how possible”; Beethoven-Hegel—nirvana won through devastating struggle.

For Mozart, I think it’s “post-Hegelian.” In Hegel’s system, “absolute” is living unity containing all contradictory movements within itself. Mozart’s presentation can to some extent be understood as the state after dialectic reaches completion—after absolute spirit achieves self-knowledge, a playful, transparent freedom. In him, subject and object, individual and whole, are not reconciled through struggle, but are already in reconciliation from the start.

Human emotions are complex, life has no solutions. Mozart writes death (Requiem), loneliness (Piano Concerto No. 23 adagio), and disorder and mockery under elegance (The Marriage of Figaro). He knows where pain lies.

His genius and restraint: he refuses to magnify individual pain into a philosophical proposition music must resolve. A sad melody can be instantly pierced by a bright modulation; an uneasy harmony finds balance in the next phrase. Not all profundity must come at the cost of strangling contradiction and completing thought.

Understanding the world—thinking chooses Hegel.

Living in the world—music chooses Mozart.
