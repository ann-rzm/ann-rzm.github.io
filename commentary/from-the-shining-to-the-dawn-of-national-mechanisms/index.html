From The Shining to the Dawn of National Mechanisms

Abstract — To avoid pedantry and arrogant soliloquy, and due to length, theories are extremely simplified and not rigorous. The Shining is an interesting aesthetic film. Elegant visuals, refined music, benevolent life forms create a slowly hollowed-out non-human atmosphere for the hotel.

To avoid pedantry and arrogant soliloquy, and due to length, theories are extremely simplified and not rigorous.

The Shining is an interesting aesthetic film. Elegant visuals, refined music, benevolent life forms create a slowly hollowed-out non-human atmosphere for the hotel. Similarly, Silent Hill’s opening: first quiet, then visibility decreases, boundaries blur, direction is gradually lost. Importantly, you realize the fog will never clear. When the world stops giving feedback, there’s a cold dampness, a familiar but unplaceable chill—making you feel you’ve touched something but can’t get a response, leaving you isolated from the world. Thus, lost in distortion, sinking in collapse.

Jack’s actions largely don’t require supernatural explanation. When a person unknowingly placed in a resource-limited, isolated, closed environment—they often forget so-called decency and morality. Extreme scenarios merely amplify what already exists. (Of course, darkness or altruism are both possible; in this case, darkness has higher risk of amplification.)

As a human, first live, second don’t die, third live well. Formally: biologically, humans have three basic needs—survival, harm avoidance, resource maximization. As for evil or good nature, I won’t debate that. However, if mentioning “goodness”: is a baby good? They lack capacity for evil. Is a strong person “good”? Without conflicts of interest, they needn’t risk evil. Similarly, “big shots” often appear approachable.

So-called “morality” from an evolutionary perspective is a behavioral pattern repeatedly proven most cost-effective in long-term human needs game—a result, a comprehensive optimal solution. (*This is an extremely simplified explanation of moral origins; description ≠ legitimacy.)

First, humans aren’t good at repeatedly calculating optimal solutions; without a universal axiom, every interaction would require recalculation and re-game—extremely costly, with redundant interference like bias and impulse. So defaults are needed, later defined as “morality.”

Why defaults? Simplest example: how a top student ascends the throne. Initially, their answers seem novel, effective for scoring—follow them to survive. Further, the divinity’s status solidifies, so their answers are defaulted as correct. If this person truly is a reincarnated Wang Bo, a few more titles make questioning rare—even feeling they radiate wisdom. Thinking answers “should” be as they say—no explanation needed. Very convenient, ready-made points.

Humans instinctively hate unpredictability. Cooperation makes behavior predictable; trust makes costs controllable. Thus, for the whole, “morality” being esteemed has nothing to do with individual character—it reduces noise and variables. “Goodness” is often explained as noble—how define noble? Often because it’s convenient, stable, replicable—repeated often. If constantly saying “cost-effective,” you’re repeatedly mentioning game, so it needs packaging—packaged as “justice,” “goodness,” “virtue.” What a constellation of human brilliance. But of course, this brilliance exists because it illuminates the largest range of people, so they voluntarily revere it. More reverence, local organization needed—thus “civilization.”

Conversely, some violate morality. Colonial history, predatory history. And these “bandit logics” can repeat—because they find them most beneficial. Take China’s “Jingkang Humiliation”: famine, war, bones in the wild. In such environments, people are no longer “people,” but “enemies,” “animals,” “objects.” This extreme environment unleashes hidden sides.

Similarly, strong civilizations inevitably repeatedly plunder. Internally: internal conflict is costly, so emphasize rules and cooperation (though degrees of looseness and mobility vary by region). Externally: the weak before the strong are low-cost, controlled-risk—when benefits are huge, plunder is rational. In all eras, whether micro individuals or macro collectives, this expansion is inevitable. Why else did ancient rulers always pursue “unifying the world”? Only differences in priority, capability, and checks. This also means once a civilization becomes a “fault” superpower—unchecked by others—it risks becoming “insane.”

Thus, facing oppression risk, relying only on “everyone willing” is not binding. If any outlier exists, rule-followers suffer (though short-term). Top-down desires fail; reverse coercion is needed. Thus a third party outside conflicts, capable of monopolizing violence and enforcing punishment—the state, law.

From a legal perspective, “people obey laws because they believe they have legitimacy”—procedural justice, being respected—this is the advanced form of the earlier driver. Through large-sample research, Tyler confirms this. Let’s derive theoretically how this conjecture is constructed:

Assume no effective rules, at least two rational individuals contesting a scarce resource—conflict easily arises.

Two scenarios: A. No third party, B. Third party exists.

In A, without third party, private resolution. Two methods: a1 endure, a2 retaliate. a1 risks recurrence; a2 has subtypes—random retaliation has endless consequences, so effective retaliation must be certain, predictable, and “publicized” to establish authority—i.e., certain, stable, public.

Who guarantees this ideal model? If the retaliator does it themselves, “effective” conditions may not be met. So it’s outsourced to a relatively fixed subject. This title might be “Sister Wang” (rural China) or “Secretary Zhang” (legal China). Ideally, “Sister Wang wearing Secretary Zhang’s hat.”

But if bypassing the third party, continuing private retaliation—who ensures authority? Declare private retaliation illegal, theoretically cutting off wrongdoing. Thus, private retaliation upgrades to public “retaliation” (adjudication). So why do people obey rules? Because if you break the law, the third party retaliates—you suffer.

This is deterrence-based legal order, punishment as primary driver. But management is troublesome; as society complexifies—membership and behavior types—management costs rise sharply. Deterrence alone is too costly. So must upgrade from the root—obedience willingness: make obeying “beneficial to the individual”—interest-based legal order. But note: upgrade means deterrence becomes back-up, not upfront. More and more find law-abiders benefit more (cooperation, transaction)—trust becomes internalized.

Now introduce two authorities: Rousseau and Machiavelli—the former more N (abstract), the latter more S (executable).

About the former: social contract’s essence is complete surrender and monopoly of retaliation rights. About the latter: order’s threat is those who disregard rules—irrelevance, not opposition; because internal violators at least acknowledge rules, but disregard denies rules themselves.

Why does “an anarchist die accidentally”? Neither morality nor law kills them.

Rousseau would say they’re logically unprotected—the system only serves citizens. In discussions of the general will, “whoever refuses to obey the general will will be forced to be free by the whole community” (*debated, not fully agreed—background only). So it’s not execution; laws only execute citizens—those outside the contract are not processed. But note: “forcing” is a change of identity attitude; refusing the general will equals excluding oneself from the community, thus losing its protection.

Machiavelli in The Prince says humans are ungrateful, fickle, self-interested. This is an assumption—people in uncertainty choose short-term security. The book discusses behavior’s marginal effect on the state—whether it leads to death. Criminals deviating within the system can be punished, co-opted; but those denying institutional legitimacy—you can’t predict their next move—they might catch the ball or kick it back. In Discourses on Livy, his summary of Rome: Rome’s freedom was built on ruthlessness toward “order-breakers”—corruptors.

Actually both discuss different sides of the same question, purely objective theoretical derivation—not about “crime and punishment” or “just/unjust”—not about “people” but about “managing people.” Thus, for that “anarchist,” both might say “accidental death”—not homicide; he was simply exposed to natural risk.

Similarly, this explains how states grow this way. Some societies face persistent external threats; some have highly concentrated resources; some have loose borders and migratory freedom; some have extremely high population density... These lead to constraints forming—early or late, strong or weak, centralized or decentralized. Natural resources, complex challenges and advantages, historical backgrounds, cooperation environments—together push large collectives toward, derive stable solutions—institutions.

Returning to Silent Hill. Initially no malice is visible, yet coldness is felt. Because the world is indifferent, unresponsive—but consequences still exist. Fear can be disguised as civilization, or something else. But civilization remains legitimate long-term because it indeed benefited the most people under the conditions of its time.

At the end of Life of Pi, Ang Lee’s line: “Which is the better story?” Order has rules, meaning, the world responds to you. But reality certainly has predatory power, certainly “no response.” Why can some “smoke bombs” through threat transform this disregard for rules, bullying of the weak, into their own glory? Because this unpredictable posture can at any moment turn the former into the latter. When the latter is too naked, repeatedly proven possible bloody—“submission,” choosing to believe the tiger, is to survive.

Life sometimes has unbearable weight; people choose narratives. But if narratives themselves become too light, people must face reality.

Pi never forgot the killing, solitude, and fear at sea. But when all was calm, he chose to tell the writer a myth—with orange waves, glowing whales, carnivorous island, and a Bengal tiger named Richard Parker.
