The so-called love-obsessed has almost nothing to do with love itself. It’s not a psychological flaw, not trauma after-effect; not simply “loving too deeply.” That is, love-obsession has nothing to do with “naivety,” “need for love,” “dependence”—those sentimental “tears before words.” It’s being too easily embedded in a ready-made story—having excessive narrative compliance.

This type’s core feature stems from thinking structure problems, not emotional richness or high sensitivity. They are easily captured and driven by a tension-filled, classic love narrative template. That is, rather than “loving someone,” they quickly and thoroughly insert themselves into the roles of “devoted lover” and “wronged party.”

Typical behaviors include but aren’t limited to: excessive giving, anxious waiting, painful indulgence—reaching a chaotic itch and sticky squirming. Participants faithfully play the script assigned by this narrative. Love’s premise is “seeing”—subject-to-subject relationship; thus seeing the other as an independent, free, possibly departing “person”—therefore naturally containing clear awareness of “letting go.” So they don’t love the other—they love the “deeply loving” self in the story; this is narrative addiction, dependence, subject-object relationship.

Modern people experience love often “second-hand.” Zhang Ailing said, “We who grow up in urban culture always see paintings of the sea before the sea. Read love novels before knowing love.” (I myself only started thinking about this after reading that sentence.)

When a person with high narrative compliance enters a relationship, they’re not feeling a real, complex, concrete person—they’re “finding a role.” Stendhal in The Red and the Black precisely describes this state: “In Paris, love is the child of novels; men and women entering love immediately check themselves against a character in a certain book.” From a richly stocked narrative template library, they select the plot and role matching their situation, then begin imitating. Most entertaining example: the earlier “Qiong Yao dramas” that swept China.

Pain is fuel for maintaining narrative.

Under this narrative drive, so-called “being abused” is an active, functional path. Because it’s the lowest-cost way to gain meaning. The abused often don’t want to face real imbalance, don’t want to face “the other never promised me,” don’t want to face the relationship’s emptiness and contingency. Thus, for narrative addicts, predictable pain is more “comfortable,” “profound,” and “artistic” than total neglect’s void.

So-called “zombie revival” is liquidating old narrative surplus value.

The Red and the Black is often misread as a love novel because it has several surface romantic elements: adultery, passion, drama, push-pull. But on the contrary, Stendhal writes about “why love cannot happen in modern society”—a negative novel.

If “love-obsessed” is embedding oneself in a ready-made love narrative, then Julien shows a seemingly extreme opposite. He’s not a simple opportunist. His problem is more serious. He has extremely high self-evaluation, yet lives in a social structure that completely denies his legitimacy.

He is a carpenter’s son from a poor peasant family. His first scene: his father knocks the book from his hands. Thus, Julien never forgets his humble origins. “I am a person who at the bottom of the society, and this upper-class society is incompatible with me.”

He considers himself a Natural Aristocrat. Actually, in its original Greek conception, this term was purer—from Aristos (best people) and Kratia (power, rule). Simply put, its ideal: the most excellent, virtuous, wise should rule the community—“select the worthy.” So when he finds himself barred from hereditary aristocracy, and sees the “nobility” around him are mostly vulgar, hypocritical, birth-based freeloaders—he despises this dead hereditary system. Thus a sharp, complex emotion emerges.

The title is Red and Black. What is red? What is black? Why “Red and Black” not “Black and Red”?

In France at that time, officers‘ uniforms were red; priests’ robes black. Julien’s dream was always red. However, due to historical facts, social turmoil—no way into the military. But then Napoleon crowned himself, dominating Europe. From a humble, poor junior officer, relying only on his sword—he stood at the world’s peak.

But Napoleon failed. The red disappeared. This imagined self-realization was torn apart.

Thus black appeared. Julien didn’t know Latin, but memorized the entire Bible to fake prodigy status, obtaining a position in the mayor’s household. He infiltrated the only remaining authority—the church—imitating its language, just for a temporary identity to survive.

Red: the “true self” he craved, but path blocked.

Black: the false, compromised exile path, but must play, to survive.

So he found a third path.

Julien’s feelings for the mayor’s wife are highly dramatized and continuously dependent on imagination. He didn’t love Madame de Rênal, but temporarily lodged his “I am recognized” in her gaze—through her, experiencing “class ascent.” So when the relationship threatens his self-narrative, he immediately turns to attack.

The mayor’s wife isn’t “love-obsessed.” On the contrary, she hasn’t learned to perform love. She hasn’t read Rousseau-style love novels; her emotional experience isn’t pre-formatted by romantic narratives. So her love is unguarded. This “love” in modern society is most easily used and sacrificed.

Self-transcendence is human instinct—needs an outlet. When one cannot achieve self-transcendence through war, politics, public sphere, these energies pour into the most private relationships. Thus “love” is forced to bear functions it can’t handle: proving value, identity status, historical compensation. When love is forced to take over the meaning functions once held by politics and religion, group changes follow social factors (and feed back). This is why 19th-century novels began writing extensively about adultery.

In this sense, “love-obsession” is an existential problem, a value-definition problem—not just, or even about, emotion. Love-obsession’s logic is “as long as I still hurt because of you, I haven’t been ignored by the world.” So it’s not masochism as the goal; being affected is the goal. For example, Julien is the initiator of this relationship structure.

Again, this isn’t a moral issue. Stendhal shows a necessity—structural. When the world doesn’t give legitimate great outlets, “love” is forced into crossfire. When one can only confirm self-worth through “being loved” (object) or “conquering” (making the other object), meeting and resonance between two independent souls based on true seeing are no longer possible.
